A car doing a mid-air flip is ridiculous — in the best possible way. Tesla’s new Roadster demonstration looks like someone has been allowed to build the science-fiction idea rather than politely leave it in a sketchbook.[1]

On the 15th of October 2026, Tesla showed the car launching from an inverted ramp, rolling through the air and landing on its wheels.[1]
That is properly exciting. It also leaves a fairly reasonable question: what on earth would owning one involve?

Beyond the price, the useful questions are where you could use it, what the demo proves and whether it is ready for customers.

## Why this matters

The appeal is easy to understand. A machine doing something unexpected can make the future feel less like another software update. There is room to enjoy that without immediately demanding that it carry a week's shopping.

Elon Musk, Tesla’s chief executive, has announced a production target of 2028.[1]

## The main idea

### What actually happened

CBS describes the Roadster clinging to an upside-down section of ramp before launching and rotating in the air.[1]
Aviation Week, the aerospace publication, describes the airborne manoeuvre as a short hop using cold-gas thrusters developed with SpaceX, Musk’s rocket company.[3]

Here, “thrusters” means devices that push the vehicle using jets of gas; CBS reports that Musk described ultra-high-pressure air tanks.[1]

Business Insider also reports that the spectacular stunts were performed without a driver inside, for safety reasons.[2]
It reports Musk’s own qualification that the vehicle was not necessarily optimised for safety.[2]

### Which Roadster?

The name covers different proposed versions.[2]
Business Insider reports that Tesla describes Performance as “road ready”, Thrust as “race ready”, and the flying Rocket version as invitation-only.[2]

The Performance version’s reported fan-suction system is another part of the story: Tesla describes it as improving performance on and off the track.[2]

### Could you use it on the road?

The flying Rocket version is currently not approved for public-road use, according to Tesla’s warning reported by CBS and Business Insider.[1][2]

There is also a surprising detail. The Federal Aviation Administration, the US aircraft regulator, told CBS that this Tesla does not meet its definition of an aircraft.[1]

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the US road-vehicle safety regulator, told CBS that the manufacturer must meet applicable federal vehicle standards and drivers must obey local traffic laws.[1]

### What about the EU?

The European Commission describes type approval as checks that a vehicle model meets EU safety, environmental and production requirements before it is authorised for the market.[4]
Those checks cover things such as braking, lights, stability control and crash protection.[4]

For a European buyer, the useful question is which exact version is approved and for what use. The sources checked for this article do not establish EU approval for Rocket or permission for its airborne use.

### What would it cost?

Business Insider reports that the final purchase price has not been determined.[2]
Reservation payments are credited towards the eventual cost rather than fixing that cost, according to its review of Tesla’s terms.[2]
Its report puts the Performance reservation at $50,000.[2]
That is a reservation figure in US dollars for Performance, not the purchase price of the flying Rocket version.[2]

The same report says the reservation payment is refundable.[2]
It says final pricing will be confirmed closer to production, while options, features and hardware are still being worked out.[2]

The wait has a history: Business Insider reports that the previous next-generation Roadster was unveiled in 2017 and never entered production in that form.[2]
Its review says the new reservation agreement does not guarantee either when delivery will happen or whether it will happen.[2]

### The ownership questions

Running costs are the other part of the price. Servicing, insurance and access to suitable places to use the car would all belong in an ownership budget.

Instead, picture the ownership routine: finding a qualified repairer, arranging cover for the intended activity and knowing what happens after a fault. Those are useful things to ask about before paying for the exciting bit.

Then ask where you would actually enjoy it. An occasional, controlled demonstration might be enough for a collector. Someone expecting to shorten a daily journey needs a much stronger case.

## What people usually get wrong

A short airborne hop is a different achievement from a flight across town; the manoeuvre Aviation Week describes is the former.[3]

That does not make an impractical machine pointless. A showpiece can be worth building for the challenge, the experience or sheer curiosity; it does not have to solve congestion to be interesting.

## Dadbot take

I think this is amazing. A car that can perform a controlled airborne trick is considerably more interesting than another screen on the dashboard.

The question is what it is amazing *as*. As an engineering demonstration, it earns the attention. As an everyday transport proposal, it still has questions to answer about permission, repeatability, support and cost.

For someone who wants a remarkable machine and has somewhere appropriate to enjoy it, practicality may not be the main point. Fair enough. Part of the appeal of a machine like this is that it is extraordinary.

## Practical takeaway

Before judging a flying-car claim, look for five separate answers:

- **The version:** which vehicle actually has the advertised capability?
- **The demonstration:** what happened, under what conditions, and with whom inside?
- **The permission:** where is that exact activity allowed?
- **The money:** is the quoted figure a deposit, an estimate or a final price?
- **The ownership:** who services, insures and supports it afterwards?

## Final thought

There is something wonderful about seeing a science-fiction idea become a real machine. Even a flying car eventually needs somewhere to park.
