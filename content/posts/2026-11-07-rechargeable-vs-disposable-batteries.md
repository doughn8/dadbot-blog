A battery drawer should earn its keep. It needs the right sizes, a few charged spares and fewer mystery cells that might have one last remote-control click in them.

Switch and Click's video, *Why I'm Never Buying Regular Batteries Ever Again*, makes an enthusiastic case for rechargeables, describing research and household flashlight tests.[7]
What does the power actually cost once you include the charger, replacement packs and everyday faff?

## Why this matters

The cheapest pack is easy to spot. Better value depends on how long each set works, how often you replace it and whether charging fits your routine.

Energizer's technical handbook describes nickel-metal hydride rechargeables, usually called NiMH, as a good fit for frequently used devices that need plenty of power.[1]
Its alkaline guide describes batteries for devices that use a little power at a time, such as remote controls and clocks.[2]

## The main idea

### Start with the battery type

AA and AAA describe sizes; the label tells you the battery chemistry and whether it is rechargeable.[4]
Ordinary alkaline disposables are used once.[6]
NiMH cells are designed to be charged and used repeatedly.[1]
Single-use lithium batteries are another non-rechargeable type, so check the label rather than relying on the word “lithium”.[4]
This comparison concentrates on ordinary alkaline cells and NiMH rechargeables for household AA/AAA devices.

For a concrete example, IKEA lists a LADDA AA rechargeable rated at 1.2 volts and 2450mAh, and describes toys, torches and cameras among its higher-consumption uses.[5]
Voltage is the electrical push available to the device.[1]
Capacity, in milliamp-hours or mAh, describes how much charge the battery can deliver under stated conditions.[2]

### Why 1.2 volts can still do the job

NiMH cells hold fairly steady around 1.2 volts during use; alkaline voltage gradually falls as the cell runs down.[1][2]
A device stops working when the battery voltage falls below the level it needs, even if some charge remains.[2]
That stopping point is called its cut-off voltage.[2]

Energizer gives an example of an AA alkaline supplying a steady 100 milliamps of current: it delivers about 2500mAh down to 0.8 volts, but about 1500mAh if the test stops at 1.2 volts.[2]
The same cell gives different usable capacities depending on when the test stops.[2]

Alkalines also tend to deliver more charge when a device draws power slowly.[2]
Energizer's NiMH guide says rechargeables can outperform alkalines in demanding applications.[1]
Check which battery type your device allows, then compare how long suitable sets actually keep it working.[6]

### When does the charger pay for itself?

Take an illustrative four-battery device. Use these assumed prices and charging figures, substituting your own local costs:

- Four rechargeable cells cost €10.
- A compatible charger costs €15.
- Each disposable costs €0.50, making a replacement set €2.
- Charging four cells uses 0.02 kilowatt-hours (kWh) at the wall, including charging losses, at €0.40 per kWh.

The rechargeables and charger cost €25 upfront. Each full charge adds €0.008, or 0.8 euro cents. After 13 uses, including a charge for each use, the total is about €25.10. Buying 13 disposable sets costs €26.

After 50 uses, the totals are €25.40 for rechargeables and €100 for disposables. In this example, buying the charger and cells accounts for most of the rechargeable cost.

These sums assume both types keep the device working for the same time. If a rechargeable set runs for fewer hours, include the extra charges needed to match the disposable set. If it runs longer, give it credit for that extra use.

At €0.20 per disposable cell, with everything else unchanged, rechargeables first become cheaper on the 32nd use, assuming both types last equally long. Owning a suitable charger already removes the assumed €15 purchase from your calculation.

The example charges the whole cost of the charger to one set of batteries. If several devices share it, count the charger once, then add all the cells you need. Include spare sets: being able to swap immediately is useful, but those extra cells still cost money.

How quickly you use batteries matters too. At one set a month, 13 sets mean roughly 13 months of service. At one set a year, that stretches to 13 years. Rechargeable life depends on age, temperature, charging and storage, so check whether a slow payback is realistic.[1]

## What people usually get wrong

### A full charge and a long life are different things

Three things matter: how long a charged set runs, how much charge it keeps in storage and how many times it can be recharged.[1]
Cells gradually lose charge while sitting unused.[1]
This is called self-discharge, and heat speeds it up.[1]
For spares, check how much charge the maker says they retain in storage and under what conditions.

Energizer explains that the number of recharges achieved in controlled tests can differ from everyday use.[1]
The useful number is how many times you will use them. A large collection bought for occasional jobs spends much of its time waiting in a drawer.

Energizer says too little charging reduces performance, while overcharging can shorten the battery's life.[1]
Its care guidance requires a charger designed for the specific battery type.[6]
Choose one with clear instructions and a charging time that suits your routine.

### The environmental benefit comes from reuse

A life-cycle assessment looks at a product's impact from production through use to disposal.[3]
In a 2016 study of AA and AAA batteries, Giovanni Dolci and colleagues compared alkalines with NiMH rechargeables, including the number of recharges and the electricity used.[3]

Their model found lower impacts after 50 charging cycles across the environmental and energy measures studied, apart from damage to the ozone layer.[3]
With 20 cycles or fewer, rechargeables could come out worse on some measures of potential pollution and health impacts in their model.[3]
The environmental benefit depends on keeping the batteries in use. Buy a useful number and give them regular work.

## Dadbot take

Start with the device that keeps sending you back for batteries: a well-used toy or a compatible game controller. Try one set and a charged spare, then compare useful runtime with the disposables you normally use. For a remote that rarely needs new cells, work out whether buying a charger would repay its cost soon enough to be worthwhile.

Check the device's specified battery type and use a compatible charger, especially for alarms and other safety equipment.[6]
Keep matching sets together, store spares in a cool, dry place and keep loose cells away from metal objects that could short their terminals.[6]
When they wear out, use your local battery collection service. In the US, the Environmental Protection Agency directs rechargeables to specialist collection rather than ordinary rubbish or municipal recycling bins.[4]

Get one device working well before buying batteries for the rest of the house. The drawer can remain a drawer. It does not need its own management team.
