A children's electric toothbrush, £35, with a £35 saving.[1]
A useful purchase at half price sounds like a decent start to the Christmas shopping. But when Which?, the UK consumer organisation, checked this Oral-B Junior offer at Boots, the £70 recommended price had applied for just three days in the preceding six months.[1]
The rest of the time, the toothbrush had been £35.[1]

Quite a performance for a toothbrush. The interesting number is the price you usually pay, rather than the one with a line through it. The price histories deserve a look before the Christmas budget goes anywhere near the checkout.

## Why this matters

Black Friday falls on Friday, the 27th of November this year.[7]
Which?'s 2026 guide describes retailers launching offers weeks ahead of the day itself.[8]
For a parent trying to get presents sorted, the useful question is whether the thing on the list is available at a good price before it is needed.

A big discount needs a meaningful starting price. Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg, a German consumer organisation, warns that shops can compare an offer with the manufacturer's recommended price, which may sit well above the price normally charged.[6]
The percentage can look impressive while the actual saving over an ordinary shopping day is much smaller.[6]

That makes the Christmas list a useful place to start. Name the item, set a budget and decide what would make it worth buying. Then the offer has something sensible to compete against.

## The main idea

### A discount compared with what?

Which? followed 175 home, technology and health appliances across eight retailers from May 2024 to May 2025, covering the 2024 Black Friday event.[1]
It found that 83% were the same price or cheaper on at least one occasion outside the four-week sales period it examined.[1]

Boots told the organisation that it ran promotions throughout the year and that appearing in the Black Friday programme did not prevent a product being on offer at other times.[1]
That explanation gives the shopper a useful reason to check the ordinary price before treating a November badge as something exceptional.

The comparison to look for is the exact product's recent selling price: what it usually cost, what other shops charge and the total payable today.
A record of actual prices gives you something firmer to work with.

### The same problem, another year

For Black Friday 2022, Which? examined 208 offers across eight retailers, checking prices during the six months before and after the day.[2]
It found that 98% were available for the same price or less at another point in that period.[2]
When researchers widened the sales window to include the surrounding fortnight, 86% still matched or beat their event prices elsewhere in the year.[2]

Second-generation AirPods at Argos and Currys provide a concrete example: Which? recorded a Black Friday price of £119 against a £139 comparison price.[2]
Earlier that year, the same products had cost between £99 and £109 throughout the period it checked from late May to early September.[2]
A reduction from the more recent price produced an offer that looked better than the earlier shopping opportunity.[2]

Argos disputed how the investigation classified its offers.[2]
It said only one was a Black Friday deal and the others were part of its other promotions.[2]
Which? pointed to Black Friday wording in product URLs and the retailer's deals section.[2]

### Only a few left

Picture a shop page telling you that only two of an item remain. Suddenly, checking another shop feels like taking a risk. The offer might be gone by the time you return.

A 1975 study by Stephen Worchel, Jerry Lee and Akanbi Adewole used jars of chocolate-chip cookies to explore how availability changes perceived value.[5]
In its first experiment, female psychology students sampled cookies from jars containing either two or ten.[5]
Some saw the supply change: a fuller jar was exchanged for one with fewer cookies.[5]
Researchers also varied the explanation, saying that other participants had eaten the cookies or that the jars had been mixed up.[5]

Cookies in scarce supply received higher desirability ratings.[5]
A change from plentiful to scarce had a stronger effect than a supply that had been small all along.[5]
The explanation mattered too: depletion attributed to demand produced higher ratings than accidental depletion.[5]

This is scarcity in a recognisable form.[5]
The product stays familiar while its apparent availability changes the way it is judged.[5]
Taste ratings showed no significant difference between the conditions.[5]
In shopping terms, a dwindling stock message invites attention to what might disappear. It is worth returning attention to what the item will actually do for you.

Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg describes stock warnings and expiring offers as tactics that push shoppers to hurry.[6]
Its advice is to compare prices calmly and resist pressure from ticking clocks and shrinking bars.[6]

### The pull of free

Free is another small word that can change a decision.[4]
Researchers Kristina Shampanier, Nina Mazar and Dan Ariely tested it using a choice between chocolates, publishing their results in 2007.[4]

In one experiment, 398 people encountered a chocolate booth at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.[4]
One price combination offered a Hershey's Kiss for one cent and a Lindt truffle for fifteen cents.[4]
Another reduced both prices by one cent, making Hershey's free and Lindt fourteen cents.[4]
The gap between the two prices stayed the same.[4]

Among people who chose a chocolate, 73% selected Lindt when both options cost money.[4]
When Hershey's became free, 69% chose it instead.[4]
The researchers found that making one option free changed its appeal beyond the small reduction in its price.[4]

Another experiment put the chocolates beside a cafeteria till, adding any charge to the customer's existing bill.[4]
Customers were already paying for other purchases.[4]
The researchers again found increased demand for the free chocolate.[4]

That is a useful experiment to remember when a basket offers a free extra. Consider this imaginary checkout: the item you want costs £40, delivery is £4, and spending £50 qualifies for free delivery. An unwanted £12 extra takes the basket to £52. Paying delivery would have cost £44. The free-delivery route costs £8 more.

If the extra was already on your list, compare the combined purchase. If it was added purely to unlock the offer, look at the final total before calling it a saving.

## What people usually get wrong

A discount, a low price and good value answer different questions. The discount compares two prices. A low price needs a market comparison. Value includes whether the product suits the job you bought it for.

Which?'s current deals guide describes checking six months of market prices, considering both the average and the cheapest prices alongside product-test results.[8]
That is a more useful model for judging an offer than the size of its discount badge: price history and product quality belong together.

Those two price figures do different jobs. The cheapest recorded price shows a past opportunity; the average provides a broader comparison with the prices recorded over the period. Look at how often the item reached the price you are considering, too. That helps you decide whether the current offer suits your deadline or whether you would rather wait for another opportunity.

For a present, include the right model, accessories and delivery in your comparison. A cheaper version that misses the feature you wanted creates a different purchase. Keep the original requirement in view while comparing the totals.

## Dadbot take

Black Friday makes more sense as a marketing campaign than a reason to spend. Start with what you need and what you are happy to pay, then judge the offer against the exact model's recent prices and the full cost, including delivery and required extras.

If a countdown is doing most of the persuading, leave the page and come back to the decision. The toothbrush still needs to clean teeth and the headphones need to suit the person wearing them. Let the actual purchase earn your money.
