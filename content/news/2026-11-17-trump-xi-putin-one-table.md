Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin are due to sit down together in Shenzhen, China, on Thursday, the 19th of November, for their first three-way meeting.[1][2][4]
Nuclear weapons, wars and trade all feature in the preparations, with each government bringing its own priorities.[1][5][8]

For people outside the room, the stakes include how nuclear arsenals are controlled, who helps decide Ukraine's future and how long the current US-China trade arrangement lasts.[5][6][7]
The useful starting point is what each leader has actually put on the agenda.

## What happened

The meeting is being held alongside the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit.[2][8]
China's foreign ministry confirmed the three-way format on Monday, describing discussions about world peace and stability between major powers.[1]

US officials told ABC News they expected an initial conversation about nuclear weapons and did not anticipate a concrete agreement.[3]
Moscow, meanwhile, says diplomats are working on a possible joint statement.[8]
Those two descriptions set different expectations for the same afternoon.

## What each leader wants

### Trump: nuclear talks, Iran and Ukraine

The White House says Trump wants to discuss reducing nuclear weapons with Russia and China.[2]
On Monday, Trump also told reporters he intended to discuss Iran, economics, trade and Ukraine, according to the Guardian's account of his remarks.[5]

That last subject is a change from the earlier US briefing, which said he was not expected to raise Ukraine.[5]
Fox News also reported his intention to discuss the war, citing the Associated Press.[4]

### Xi: stability and a place at the centre

China's stated aim is stronger communication, managed differences and stable relations among the three countries.[1]
Foreign ministry spokesperson Mao Ning has described the meeting as significant while leaving its detailed agenda unpublished.[1]

Hosting also gives Xi a prominent diplomatic role. Peter Alexander, an analyst quoted by CNBC, said that bringing Trump and Putin to the table would deliver political benefits to Beijing before discussions began.[6]

### Putin: Ukraine and a joint statement

Kremlin foreign-policy aide Yury Ushakov says Putin's discussions with Trump in Shenzhen will cover Ukraine and Russian and American settlement proposals, according to Russian state news agency TASS.[8]
Ushakov also says Putin plans to discuss Ukraine with Xi separately.[8]

The Kremlin hopes the three-way meeting will produce a joint communiqué, a statement agreed by the participants.[8]
TASS reports that each leader would give his own assessment if a shared text could not be agreed.[8]
The difference would be visible in the first account issued after the talks: one agreed document or three separate versions.

## Why nuclear talks are difficult

The previous US-Russia agreement, New START, expired in February, removing binding limits on their strategic nuclear arsenals, according to the United Nations.[7]
It capped each country's deployed strategic nuclear warheads at 1,550, alongside limits on the long-range missiles and heavy bombers used to deliver them.[7]

The agreement paired those limits with data exchanges, notifications and on-site inspections designed to reduce mistrust and miscalculation.[7]
That is the practical machinery behind arms control: an agreed ceiling and ways to check what the other country is doing.

A successor arrangement would need to set out the limits and how they are checked. A timetable would establish when commitments take effect and when progress can be assessed.

China starts from a different position. Reuters reports that Beijing has resisted nuclear talks with Washington and Moscow because their arsenals are much larger, arguing that the two should speak directly first.[2]
This creates a basic negotiating problem: whether the first step should involve all three countries or begin with the two largest arsenals.

## Ukraine and Iran: who is outside the room?

Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, has warned that talks among the three powers could become a redistribution of influence, invoking the Yalta conference of 1945.[5]
The issue for Kyiv is participation in decisions about its own future.

The practical issues are who joins any subsequent negotiations and who agrees to proposed territorial or security arrangements. Those details would show how a conversation among three presidents connects to a settlement involving the country being fought over.

Iran is also on Trump's proposed agenda.[5]
The Guardian reports that China, an important economic partner of Iran, has sought to mediate in the conflict and has made avoiding renewed hostilities a priority.[5]
Washington's approach to the war and Beijing's mediation efforts provide different starting points for that discussion.[5]

For both conflicts, any announcement about further talks would need names, participants and a subject to establish what happens next.

## Trade has its own timetable

Trump and Xi are also expected to meet separately, with US officials saying they intend to focus on implementing their trade agreement.[3]
CNBC reports that the US-China trade truce is due to expire in January and that analysts largely expect an extension.[6]

That deadline gives the economic discussion a practical test. Businesses planning orders would gain a clearer planning period from an announced extension with a specified end date. A longer-term framework would raise a different question: which trading rules the two governments are prepared to keep in place.

Economist Carlos Casanova told CNBC that leaders might also assess the prospects for a more durable rebalancing of US-China trade.[6]
The bilateral trade discussion and the three-way security meeting therefore have separate jobs within the same visit.

## What is still unclear

China has left the detailed agenda open in its public briefing, while the Kremlin says work on the topics continues.[1][8]
Preparations for a separate Trump-Putin meeting are also described differently: the earlier US briefing said none was planned, while Ushakov says arrangements are being worked on.[3][8]

Whether the presidents issue a shared statement, commission further negotiations or announce a specific commitment will become clear from the meeting and their subsequent accounts. Those are the outstanding outcomes of this pre-meeting picture.

## Dadbot take

A defined next step would establish who meets again, what they negotiate and when. For nuclear arms, that means limits and a method of checking them. For conflicts, it means identifying the countries involved in any proposed negotiations. For trade, it means specific rules and dates.

Those details would make the meeting's consequences easier to assess. A follow-up process with named participants and deadlines gives people outside the room something concrete to judge once the handshakes are over.
