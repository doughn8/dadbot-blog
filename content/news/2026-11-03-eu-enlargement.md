The European Union is preparing for another round of expansion.[1]
Its executive body, the European Commission, has set out changes to help a larger Union function, while its next assessment of countries seeking membership is expected on the 4th of November.[1][4]

There are currently 27 members, with Croatia the most recent arrival in 2013.[2]
The next enlargement would bring decisions about who can work where, how businesses trade and how shared money is spent, alongside the question of who gets a seat at the table.[1][2]

## What is happening?

On the 6th of October, the Commission proposed stronger safeguards for future members, changes to decision-making and earlier participation in selected EU policies.[1]
It also promised indicative reform roadmaps for Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine, provided they maintain progress.[1]

Those are the four frontrunners identified in current reporting by Euronews and The Parliament.[4][5]
The Commission's annual assessments examine progress towards membership; Euronews reports that the new roadmaps will set milestones and be updated each year.[4]

## Who could join next?

The nine official candidates are Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Türkiye and Ukraine.[2]
Kosovo is listed separately as a potential candidate.[2]

Montenegro is closest to membership, according to Euronews, which reports that its representatives are aiming to become the EU's 28th member by 2028.[4]
The same report describes unresolved issues with neighbouring Croatia as an obstacle.[4]
Albania is also among the leaders, while Moldova and Ukraine have opened further areas of negotiation, according to The Parliament's review.[5]

Elsewhere, the obstacles differ. The Parliament reports that North Macedonia's next steps are blocked over constitutional changes recognising Bulgarian minorities, while Bosnia and Herzegovina still faces judicial requirements before formal talks can start.[5]
It describes Georgia's democratic backsliding and Türkiye's lack of progress in membership talks since 2018.[5]

The practical picture is several countries moving at different speeds. A useful way to follow it is to look at the next reform or decision each country needs, rather than imagine everyone arriving together.

## How does a country join?

The basic requirements include democratic institutions, human rights, a functioning market economy and the ability to apply EU law.[2]
The joining process, called accession, then turns those requirements into detailed checks and negotiations.[3]

First, all existing governments must agree on the framework for negotiations, through the Council of the EU, where national ministers represent their governments.[3]
The Commission examines how ready the applicant is in each policy area, known as a negotiating chapter, and recommends conditions for moving forward.[3]

A chapter covers a particular set of rules and responsibilities.[3]
The candidate has to prepare to apply and enforce those rules, including the judicial, administrative and economic reforms involved.[3]
Passing a law is one job. Having the courts and public services to make it work is another.

Every EU government must be satisfied before a chapter closes, and the negotiations finish only once all chapters are closed.[3]
The final accession treaty sets the terms, financial arrangements and any transition periods; every existing member and the applicant must sign and ratify it under their constitutional rules.[3]
The joining date is written into that treaty.[3]

## What would change for people?

The EU lists the freedom to live, study and work across member countries, access to the single market, and increased funding and investment among membership's benefits.[2]
The single market gives businesses access to a shared market governed by common rules.[2][3]

For a small business hoping to sell across a border, that could mean serving more customers under a common set of standards. For someone looking for work or a place to study, the practical attraction is a wider choice of destinations.

The terms of entry matter here. The Commission's October proposal allows transition arrangements for workers and services, agriculture and funding.[1]
These would set how particular parts of membership take effect, with the details recorded in accession treaties.[3]

Some closer cooperation can come earlier. The Commission wants candidates to participate gradually in areas including energy, transport, digital policy and defence, subject to conditions and with participation reversible.[1]
Its argument is that shared projects can deliver benefits and prepare administrations before full membership.[1]

## The EU has homework too

A bigger membership means deciding how more governments share decisions. The Commission proposes allowing groups of negotiating chapters to open, and other intermediate accession steps to proceed, without every government having to agree.[1]
Under the current process, governments must agree unanimously on starting negotiations and on closing chapters.[3]

There is also the question of what happens if reforms unravel after entry. The October package proposes stronger safeguards, potentially including suspension of Council voting rights for the most serious breaches of EU values and cooperation commitments.[1]

That creates a tension with equal membership. In May, The Guardian reported Montenegro's position that it wanted all the rights and responsibilities of an equal member, while accepting that safeguards could help sustain reforms.[6]
A safeguard needs a clear purpose, a way to judge compliance and an understandable route out of restrictions. Otherwise, the argument over entry simply follows a country inside.

Money and farming bring their own negotiations. Under the Commission's proposed 2028–2034 budget arrangements, countries would move from pre-membership funding into plans for new members, with support for areas including agriculture and regional development.[1]
For Ukraine's large agricultural sector, it proposes targeted limits on financial support and access for sensitive products.[1]
That puts a concrete choice on the table: how to welcome a major producer while managing the impact on existing farmers and shared spending.

## What is still unsettled?

Joining dates, final safeguards, transition periods and budget arrangements remain to be agreed through the relevant negotiations.[1][3]
The Parliament reports that the forthcoming roadmaps will not bind the EU to admission dates.[5]

The annual review should make the next steps clearer. Completing them will still require decisions by applicants and existing members, including domestic ratification of each accession treaty.[3]

## Dadbot take

The sensible test is whether enlargement produces a Union that works better for the people joining and those already inside. Functioning courts, usable rules and decisions that can actually be taken matter more than a handsome signing ceremony. There will be enough photographs either way.

A timetable can give reform a purpose. The stronger case for expansion is being able to explain what improves for an ordinary person when the date finally arrives.
