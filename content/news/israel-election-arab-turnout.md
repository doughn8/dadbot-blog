Arab voters have become central to Israel’s coalition talks. Following the election on the 27th of October 2026, Arab-led parties secured a record combined 19 seats in parliament, with neither main political bloc reaching a majority.[1][8]

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud party says the election was stolen through misconduct in Arab communities.[2]
Police have rejected that broad claim, while separate investigations into suspected bribery and foreign funding are under way, according to the Jerusalem Post.[2][5]

The immediate political question is who can turn those seats into a government. The other question is what investigators can actually establish about the allegations.

## What happened in the election

The Knesset is Israel’s 120-seat parliament; 61 seats provide a majority.[4]
Counting is complete: Netanyahu’s bloc has 53 seats, the main opposition 48, and Arab-led parties 19.[1][8]
The results still await certification.[1][8]
Election officials released the totals on Thursday.[1]
Gadi Eisenkot leads the main opposition bloc.[4]

That leaves both main blocs looking for support beyond their existing partners.[3]

The useful starting points are:

- Arab turnout was reported at around 80%.[4][6]
- Both main political blocs were short of a majority.[3][4]
- The official election timetable schedules publication of results for the 4th of November.[7]

A seat count supplies the numbers. Getting parties to work together supplies the government, and that second part is proving considerably harder.

## Why Arab turnout matters

JFeed reported Arab participation at 80%, against national turnout of 77.7%, the highest national figure since 1999.[6]
Those percentages become more tangible when looking at individual towns. Citing Ynet, JFeed reported turnout in Umm al-Fahm at 70.1% by 8pm, compared with 38.3% across the whole of the 2022 election day.[6]
In Sakhnin, the same report put participation at 83.5% by 8pm, against 68.4% in the previous election.[6]

By 8pm, both towns had comfortably passed their previous full-day turnout.[6]

What brought people out? AP’s reporting points to anger at Netanyahu’s government and campaign attacks portraying Arab-led parties as a threat.[1]

There is also a practical question about what stronger representation could deliver. Arik Rudnitzky, a researcher in the Israel Democracy Institute’s Arab Society programme, told Fox News that demands for supporting a government would probably include action on crime and a socioeconomic development plan for Arab communities.[3]

That gives the coalition argument a concrete meaning: policing, public spending and the conditions people live with between elections.

## What Likud is alleging

Likud’s complaint includes alleged cash payments linked to Ra’am ballot slips, intimidation of polling staff, duplicate identities and discrepancies between ballot envelopes and registered voters.[2]
The party says it is submitting hundreds of testimonies and pieces of evidence to police, the election committee and the Shin Bet, Israel’s domestic security service.[3]

Those are specific accusations which investigators can test against witnesses, records and individual polling stations. Their importance depends on what the evidence shows and how any proven misconduct affected the vote.

The police response reported on Thursday was equally specific: election day had relatively few integrity incidents, and police had no investigative or intelligence evidence supporting the sweeping theft allegations.[2]
The Jerusalem Post said evidence establishing systematic manipulation of the result had not been made public.[2]

## What the investigations cover

The Jerusalem Post reports two distinct lines of investigation. The Shin Bet is examining suspected foreign funding intended to influence voting in Arab communities, following instructions from election committee chairman Noam Sohlberg, a Supreme Court justice.[5]
His letter sought evidence to substantiate or refute the suspicions; it identified no particular countries or parties.[5]

Separately, police have arrested seven people in a suspected election-bribery case involving cash and Ra’am voting slips, the Post reported.[5]
Its account said Thursday’s police statement established no connection between that case and the suspected foreign funding.[5]

Qatar has denied interfering in the election.[1]
Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas has welcomed the interference investigation and said his party had nothing to do with the case, according to AP.[1]

The unresolved questions are the source and use of any unlawful money, the responsibility of particular people, and whether proven offences affected the allocation of seats.[5]
An election committee source told the Post that the inquiry would not delay publication of results or the president selecting someone to begin coalition-building.[5]

## Who can form a government?

This is where the campaign promises meet the parliamentary numbers. Fox’s reporting describes leading opposition parties pledging not to form a government dependent on Arab-party support, whether from inside a coalition or outside it.[3]

Those are different arrangements: joining the government puts a party inside the governing partnership; outside support lets it help sustain a government through parliamentary votes. Ra’am took part in the government formed in 2021, a decision former prime minister Naftali Bennett has defended while saying he would not repeat it, Al Jazeera reports.[4]

The Arab-led parties also have different approaches. Fox describes the Joint List as unlikely to enter a coalition, while Abbas has pursued cooperation within government to improve Arab citizens’ lives.[3]

Al Jazeera’s election explainer highlights the tension in the opposition’s position: Eisenkot had rejected the idea of depending on Arab parties, while the emerging numbers left his existing bloc below a majority.[4]

For the Joint List, leader Yousef Jabareen has publicly advocated equal Jewish-Arab partnership as the route to replacing Netanyahu’s government, according to Al Jazeera.[4]
The coalition talks will test which parties are prepared to turn their preferred outcome into an agreement.

## Dadbot take

The striking part is how voting strength and governing power can be different things. A large turnout can give representatives more seats; what they can achieve depends on who will negotiate with them and what they ask for.

The fraud claims deserve a clear account of the evidence. If particular votes were bought, establish who paid, who benefited and what changed. A claim about the whole election needs evidence that matches its scale.

Meanwhile, anyone promising to replace the government needs to explain the governing arrangement they will actually support. The electorate has supplied the seats. The politicians now have to explain how they intend to use them.
