Israel is reportedly preventing construction of a small Gaza base intended to house international troops supporting Donald Trump's reconstruction programme.[1]
The Guardian reported the delay on the 14th of October, citing a person with direct knowledge of the project.[1]

The proposed outpost is part of the security arrangements for the Board of Peace, the Trump-chaired body overseeing the planned transition and rebuilding of Gaza.[1][9]
The work it is meant to support includes housing, hospital repairs and restoring basic services—not just military facilities.[6]

## What happened

According to the Guardian, Louisiana-based Arkel International received the board's first Gaza construction contract in early August.[1]
More than two months later, contractors and trucks had not been permitted to enter and begin work.[1]

The planned fortified outpost would accommodate about 150 international troops on rotating deployments.[1]
The newspaper's source said the absence of that security presence was holding up access for the wider rebuilding programme.[1]

The Guardian said the Board of Peace, Israeli government and Israeli military declined to comment.[1]
The report did not provide an official Israeli explanation for the delay.[1]

## What the international force is meant to do

The International Stabilization Force is a temporary multinational security mission authorised by the UN Security Council, rather than the organisation responsible for running schools or repairing homes.[9]
Resolution 2803, adopted on the 17th of November 2025, allows participating countries and the Board of Peace to establish the force under a unified command acceptable to the board, working closely with Israel and Egypt.[9]

Its mandate includes protecting civilians and humanitarian operations, supporting vetted Palestinian police, securing humanitarian corridors and overseeing the process of demilitarisation—the removal of weapons and military infrastructure.[9]
The resolution also links Israeli military withdrawal to the force establishing control and stability, with agreed milestones tied to demilitarisation.[9]

That is why international troops feature in a rebuilding story. The agreed framework connects safe access, policing and the movement of goods with reconstruction, rather than treating the arrival of building materials as a separate operation.[9]

## Who would run civilian services

The civilian body is the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza, usually shortened to NCAG. In its May report to the Security Council, the Board of Peace described it as a Palestinian committee of specialists responsible for day-to-day civil administration.[8]
“Technocratic” is the term used in the plan: an administration organised around professional responsibilities such as water, electricity, health and education, rather than a military command.[8]

The same report described a sequence: weapons would be put beyond use under international verification, Israeli forces would withdraw in phases, and reconstruction would begin in certified disarmed areas under the committee's authority.[8]
The international force would help protect humanitarian operations and support the handling of surrendered weapons.[8]

These are separate jobs within the same framework. The board coordinates and oversees the transition; the Palestinian committee is intended to run civilian services; the international troops provide security support.[8]
The May document sets out the board's intended arrangements, not independent proof that they have been implemented.[8]

The resolution says the board's operating bodies are to be funded through voluntary contributions from donors, governments and its funding arrangements.[9]
UN authorisation therefore sets out responsibilities, but does not itself provide the construction budget or mean a donor's pledge has been paid.[9]

## What rebuilding would involve

On the 23rd of September, the board presented a six-month recovery proposal covering 66 programmes, with an estimated cost of $2.45 billion. The BBC reported that the proposals included housing, diesel generation and waste removal.[4]
DW's account, drawing on AP and Reuters, also listed rubble clearance, hospital repairs, economic recovery and deployment of the multinational security force.[6]

Those are practical building blocks: somewhere to live, power for essential services, usable roads and functioning medical facilities. The proposal also included restoring electricity and water infrastructure and repairing border crossings, according to DW's September reporting.[6]

The longer-term recovery estimate was about $71 billion over a decade, the BBC reported.[4]
That is a planning estimate, not a sum already spent or a reconstruction contract already delivered.[4]
At the September meeting, the board acknowledged a funding gap: the BBC reported $18 billion pledged but only $280 million received at that point.[4]

Ali Shaath, the Palestinian committee's chief commissioner, told The National that rebuilding was envisaged sector by sector, beginning in Rafah in southern Gaza before moving north.[5]
That sequence depended on Hamas relinquishing governance, weapons being decommissioned and Israeli forces withdrawing.[5]
He also rejected moving Palestinians out of Gaza and said the committee wanted residents to remain and rebuild their lives there.[5]

The National reported that the programme was aimed largely at potential international donors.[5]
Nickolay Mladenov, the board's senior representative for Gaza, described the next challenge as moving from planning to implementation while talks with Israel continued.[5]

## Why implementation is still difficult

The outpost report sits alongside a wider dispute over the order in which security steps should happen. In September, the BBC reported that Hamas had agreed to relinquish weapons in exchange for a phased Israeli withdrawal, while Israel insisted on full disarmament before withdrawing.[4]
Those positions should not be compressed into an agreement that disarmament has already happened.

Ynet also reports that Israel's security cabinet approved a limited pilot to restore basic services in July, with the first stage expected in Rafah.[2]
Its account envisages temporary accommodation and the Palestinian civilian committee operating with international security backing.[2]

Approval of a pilot and permission for a particular contractor to enter are different steps. The existence of the former does not establish that the latter has been granted.

## What remains unclear

The Guardian's source said planners expected construction to be allowed after Israel's election, scheduled for the 27th of October.[1]
That is an expectation attributed to the source, not an Israeli commitment or an established explanation of the government's motive.[1]

The sources do not establish a start date or quantify how long this outpost's delay would hold up particular civilian projects. Funding, security arrangements and access remain distinct requirements in the published reconstruction plans.[4][5]

## Dadbot take

The immediate report concerns access for a small security outpost.[1]
The wider framework connects that security presence with civilian administration and rebuilding.[1][9]
The remaining question is when the planned arrangements will become work on the ground, rather than another announced stage of the programme.
