COP31 opened in Antalya, Türkiye, on the 9th of November, with governments aiming to turn climate commitments into measures they can fund and carry out.[1]
Nearly 200 countries are taking part, and the UN climate conference is scheduled to close on the 20th of November.[1]

Finance is among the toughest issues, with developing countries seeking faster access to money for climate protection and cleaner development.[1]
Türkiye is also promoting a voluntary target to expand electricity’s role in the global energy system, with disagreement over how that power should be generated.[5]

## What happened in Antalya

Türkiye’s environment minister, Murat Kurum, took over as COP31 president at the opening, while Australia’s climate change and energy minister, Chris Bowen, will oversee the negotiations.[1]
Bowen said a presidency-led decision would be developed throughout the conference, giving countries an early start on the text rather than leaving it until the final days.[1]

COP means Conference of the Parties: the countries signed up to the UN’s climate convention.[2]
The annual meetings bring governments together to negotiate emissions cuts, finance and protection against climate impacts.[2]
The process produced the Paris Agreement in 2015, with its commitment to keep warming well below 2°C and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.[2]

This year’s emphasis is implementation: putting existing commitments into practice through national policies, investment and projects.[1][2]
Alongside the formal talks, governments, development banks, businesses and civil society work on an Action Agenda covering practical measures such as electricity networks and methane reductions.[2]

## The finance test

Developing countries want wealthier nations to fulfil their financial commitments, with disagreements continuing over who provides funding, how it is accessed and how wider financial flows support climate action.[2]

Adaptation is the part of climate policy concerned with coping with its effects, including protecting communities from heat and flooding.[1][2]
A sea wall gives a concrete example of what the funding discussions mean. Jess Gasper, the Marshall Islands’ environment minister, said it took eight years to obtain international finance for a single sea wall, according to the Guardian’s reporting from Antalya.[4]
The Pacific island country is particularly exposed to rising sea levels.[4]

Access is also a problem for smaller organisations. Cristian Barrasueta of the World Rural Forum told UN News that complicated procedures and multiple intermediaries make international climate finance difficult for small farming organisations to reach.[1]
He described families in Fiji experimenting with crop varieties and protecting seedlings against stronger cyclones.[1]
That brings the discussion down to the scale of a farm: the people adapting their crops also need a workable route to the money.

The cost of borrowing is another obstacle. Selwin Hart, the UN’s special adviser on climate action and a fair transition to cleaner economies, told UN News that financing renewable projects in developing countries can cost several times more than in wealthier countries, even with identical technology.[2]
He called for financing solutions that do not increase debt, while the UN account describes indebted governments’ limited resources for health, education and climate protection.[2]
The argument therefore concerns both the amount of funding and the terms on which countries can obtain it.

## The electricity proposal

Türkiye’s non-binding 35-by-35 Global Electrification Pledge aims for electricity to meet at least 35 per cent of global energy demand by 2035, compared with about one fifth of total final energy consumption today.[5]
In everyday terms, electrification means using electricity for more of the jobs currently done by burning fuel, including in transport, homes and industry.[6]

The European Commission’s Electrify Now initiative separates that work into three areas: electric technologies in transport, buildings and industry; electricity networks and storage; and additional clean generation.[6]
Its explanation links reliable delivery to stronger grids and a more flexible power system, alongside the extra electricity supply.[6]

Kurum argues that this shift can strengthen energy security, reduce exposure to volatile import markets and cut emissions.[5]
At the opening, he also said families should feel the effect of climate decisions through lower energy bills, according to UN News.[1]

The proposal has drawn criticism from climate advocates because it does not require the additional electricity to come from low-carbon sources, according to the AFP and Reuters report published by Al Jazeera.[5]
Pacific island countries are among those seeking a clearer global route away from fossil fuels, while oil and gas producers including Saudi Arabia argue those fuels can remain a substantial part of the energy mix when paired with technologies that reduce or capture emissions.[5]

Fatih Birol, head of the International Energy Agency, told the Guardian that an endorsed electrification target could send a signal to markets and create an opportunity to increase renewable and nuclear electricity.[4]

## The other decisions on the table

Countries also need to work out how a mechanism supporting the shift to cleaner economies will operate, after agreeing to establish it last year.[2]
This is the “just transition” discussion: how the move to cleaner economies is supported, including the finance needed to make it possible.[2]

Adaptation negotiations cover how to measure progress as well as developing countries’ demands for greater financial support.[2]
COP31 will also launch the second global stocktake, the Paris Agreement’s process for assessing countries’ collective progress towards its long-term goals.[2]

## What remains undecided

The presidency-led decision’s content and ambition remain under negotiation, with Bowen acknowledging differences over how far countries will go.[1]
Support for the voluntary electrification proposal and the treatment of the electricity’s sources are also live issues.[4][5]
Finance discussions still have to address responsibilities and access, while countries must settle how the transition mechanism will work.[2]

These are separate pieces of the conference’s work: the negotiated decision, voluntary initiatives and national commitments each have their own role in the process.[2]

## What happens next

The European Commission’s programme lists a global electrification ministerial for the 10th of November, followed by a leaders’ summit on electrification and a methane ministerial on the 11th.[3]
City-focused energy events follow on the 12th.[3]

Formal negotiations are scheduled to continue until the 20th of November, with the presidency seeking a consensus decision on practical climate action.[1]
The immediate task is to close the differences already identified over finance, emissions action and support for the transition.[1][2]
