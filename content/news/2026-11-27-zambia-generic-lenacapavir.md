The first shipments of a cheaper version of the six-month HIV prevention medicine lenacapavir reached Zambia on the 25th of November 2026.[1][3]
The Associated Press reported that the medicine was expected to go into use within three weeks, once it had cleared the country’s checks.[3]

Zambia already had a rollout under way for the original medicine.[9]
The new deliveries bring generic supply into that programme.[1][9]

## What arrived in Zambia

In September, Zambia’s health ministry received a shipment of the original lenacapavir at the University Teaching Hospital in Lusaka, the capital.[9]
The Global Fund, an international partnership that finances programmes against HIV, tuberculosis and malaria, announced the first generic deliveries almost three months later.[1][3][9]
The fund and the US government are financing purchases as both branded and generic supplies go out to countries during the transition.[1][2]

In November, the Global Fund reported that more than 189,000 people across ten countries that introduced the drug early had received a first dose.[1]
The United Nations programme on HIV and AIDS, known as UNAIDS, said Zambia’s rollout aimed to reach 82,000 people across 542 selected health facilities by the end of 2026.[9]

UNAIDS estimated that 23,000 people acquired HIV in Zambia in 2025.[9]
The agency reported that adolescents and young adults accounted for more than a third of those new infections.[9]

## What a six-month visit changes

A person who might be exposed to HIV can take medicine beforehand to lower their chance of acquiring it.[4][5]
That is pre-exposure prophylaxis, usually shortened to PrEP.[4][5]
One way of taking it is with a pill every day, whether that’s a workday, a weekend away or a morning when the routine has gone out of the window.[4]

With lenacapavir, the first visit includes injections under the skin and a short course of tablets to start the medicine.[4]
The maintenance visits are about six months apart.[4]
The World Health Organization recommends HIV testing when people start and continue long-acting prevention.[5]
Its guidance includes rapid diagnostic tests, so offering the injection need not depend on a clinic arranging specialised laboratory testing for every visit.[5]
For someone switching from daily pills, the job shifts from remembering a tablet each morning to getting to a clinic and being able to go back six months later.[4][5]

The trial reports reviewed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found that reactions at the injection site were common and mostly mild or moderate, including soreness and lumps under the skin.[4]
The lumps can stay for several months, sometimes for more than a year.[4]
Few participants stopped taking lenacapavir because of reactions at the injection site.[4]

## What the trials found

The first big trial, called PURPOSE 1, enrolled girls and young women aged 16 to 25 in South Africa and Uganda.[4]
At the main analysis, there were no new infections among the 2,134 participants in the lenacapavir group.[4]
The study also followed participants assigned to daily pills and estimated how often people in the screened population acquired HIV without PrEP.[4]

PURPOSE 2 enrolled a different group, primarily men, across seven countries including South Africa, Brazil and Thailand.[4]
Its main analysis found two infections among 2,179 people in the lenacapavir group, a rate 96% lower than the study’s estimated background rate without PrEP.[4]
The infection rate was also lower than in the daily-pill group in that trial.[4]
Researchers got the daily-pill comparison by randomly assigning participants to one group or the other.[4]
They estimated the background rate separately from the population screened for the trial.[4]

The World Health Organization recommends offering the injection as one of several prevention choices.[5]

## What US$40 buys

The Gates Foundation and Indian drugmaker Hetero announced a deal to make the generic injections available for about US$40 per person per year after the short course of starter tablets.[6]
The annual figure is for the medicine supplied to health programmes.[6]
Getting it to a clinic, testing people and paying for staff time are part of running the service.[5][10]

Gilead Sciences, the maker of the original version, gave six manufacturers royalty-free licences to make lenacapavir for 120 low- and middle-income countries.[6]
The Gates Foundation put money up front and backed the Hetero deal with a promise to buy a certain volume, giving the manufacturer a basis for investing in production.[6]

A separate agreement announced by global health agency Unitaid sets a US$40 annual price for the injection made by another Indian company, Dr. Reddy’s Laboratories, for the countries covered by Gilead’s licences.[7]
Hetero said it planned to send 300,000 vials across Zambia, Uganda and Zimbabwe in its initial shipments.[8]
It expects its own starter tablets in January 2027 and says Gilead will supply the tablets in the meantime, subject to local requirements.[8]

## How the shot gets to clinics

The Global Fund’s November announcement said Uganda’s delivery was expected in the following days and that a shipment it was financing for Zimbabwe was due in early 2027.[1]
Uganda had already received an initial 19,200 doses of the branded injection in February, according to Ugandan newspaper New Vision.[2]

The Global Fund and the US government plan to deliver four times as much lenacapavir in 2027 as in 2026.[1]
Their plan calls for more generic shipments in the first quarter of 2027 alongside continued supplies of the branded medicine.[1]

How far is the nearest place to get a shot? In Zambia, Unitaid says distance, limited clinic hours and concerns about privacy get in the way of people using prevention services.[10]
A project led by Zambia’s University Teaching Hospital and health ministry plans to link health facilities in ten districts where HIV is common with places closer to where people live and work.[10]
Its plans include pharmacies, community health posts and drop-in centres.[10]
Community health workers are part of the plan too, along with tools to help teams follow up with people after an appointment.[10]
The project will compare how well the different delivery options work and what they cost, so the health ministry can decide which ones to expand.[10]
