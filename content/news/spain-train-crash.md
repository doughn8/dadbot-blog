At least 68 people have died after two high-speed trains collided near Urda, in Spain’s Toledo province, on the morning of the 28th of October 2026, according to regional authorities.[1][4]
Dozens more were injured, and rescue crews have been searching the damaged carriages for people still inside.[2][4]
RTVE’s regional update said 15 people were seriously injured.[4]

The trains were carrying passengers from Madrid towards Seville.[5]
Within hours, the response stretched from the trackside to hospitals, station assistance points and a sports pavilion prepared to receive injured passengers in Urda.[2][4][5]

## What happened near Urda

A Renfe train struck the rear of an Iryo train that had stopped on the line, causing carriages to derail, the Associated Press reported.[2]
The collision happened in a rural area between Urda and Los Yébenes, south of Madrid.[2]
Both services were travelling in the same direction.[2]

The organisations involved have different jobs:

- **Renfe** is Spain’s state-owned train operator; **Iryo** is a private passenger operator.[2]
- **Adif** manages the railway infrastructure and traffic, rather than operating either passenger service.[1][5]
- High-speed services between Madrid and Andalusia were suspended after the collision.[3][5]

Those different jobs matter when tracing who knew a train was stopped and who authorised another to move.
Spain’s transport minister, Óscar Puente, has described a computer communications failure followed by an instruction to restart the Renfe service.[1][4]

## The rescue and the people helping

Emergency workers have been concentrating on the most badly damaged carriages, with injured passengers taken to hospitals in the surrounding area, according to health minister Mónica García.[2]
Ambulances and medical helicopters travelled to and from the site, and a military emergency brigade joined firefighters, police and other responders.[2]

RTVE, Spain’s public broadcaster, reported that around 200 members of the military emergency unit were deployed, alongside more than 300 Civil Guard officers.[4]
The military teams’ tasks included finding trapped people, opening access routes and moving heavy railway wreckage.[4]
Police were helping with rescues, moving injured people and identifying those who had died.[4]

Help also came from people nearby.[4]
Passenger Fernando Seco told RTVE that people working in the fields opened holes in the fence so passengers could leave the railway, and that he and others helped the injured after getting out.[4]
Other survivors described local drivers taking people to the pavilion.[4]

Urda’s mayor, Manuel Galán, said civil protection officials had asked the town to prepare that sports pavilion to receive injured passengers.[2]
Survivors who were unhurt were taken away by bus.[2]

For families, the response includes finding out who has been taken where.[4]
RTVE reported that the Civil Guard had opened offices for close relatives to provide information and DNA samples to help identify missing people.[4]
Adif announced assistance points at Madrid-Puerta de Atocha, Sevilla-Santa Justa and Córdoba-Julio Anguita, later adding Málaga-María Zambrano.[5]
Renfe and Iryo also activated arrangements to support affected passengers and families.[1][4]

## What the transport minister says

Puente’s account puts the decision to restart the train at the centre of the investigation.[1][4]
He said a computer communications failure affected the railway’s safety system and automatically brought traffic to a stop.[1]
RTVE reported his explanation that three computers had lost communication across a 63-kilometre stretch of line.[4]

According to the minister, the control centre then authorised the Renfe train to continue while a red signal remained active and the Iryo train was stopped ahead.[1][4]
He described that instruction as an erroneous decision and a breach of protocol.[1]
AP reported that Puente said the train had been authorised to resume without speed restrictions; he said the drivers were not to blame.[2]

Renfe’s response is that its train continued under the authorisation it had received.[1]
In the statement reported by Reuters, the operator said investigators would need to establish the exact sequence, the traffic conditions and the circumstances of the impact.[1]
Iryo said it had activated its safety protocols and was working with the authorities.[2]

This is the minister’s initial explanation, with the formal investigation still under way.[4]
The key sequence to establish is the communications failure, the information available to the control centre and the instruction that followed.

## What the investigation needs to establish

Spain’s railway accident investigation commission, known as the CIAF, has opened an investigation, RTVE reported.[4]
The broadcaster also reported that the Civil Guard had questioned the traffic-control worker responsible for the line at the time.[4]

The inquiry needs to answer some straightforward questions. What did the control centre know about the stationary train? Who authorised the restart, on what information and with what restrictions? How was that instruction communicated to the driver? Those answers need to fit together into a clear timeline.

The train’s actual speed at impact also remains to be established.[2]
Puente said investigators were studying the information available to determine how fast it had been travelling.[2]
For a full explanation, the inquiry will also need to show which safeguards should have kept the trains apart and what changes follow from its findings.

## Why January’s crash is part of this story

Spain had already suffered a fatal collision involving the same two operators in January, when 46 people died near Adamuz in Córdoba province.[2][4]
In that crash, an Iryo train derailed into the neighbouring track and collided with an oncoming Renfe service.[4]
The January crash remains under investigation, according to Reuters’ reporting carried by RTÉ.[1]

The two events have different reported sequences: January involved a derailment into an oncoming train; the Urda collision involved one train striking another from behind.[2][4]

Speaking at the scene, prime minister Pedro Sánchez promised a thorough investigation and action if negligence was established, RTVE reported.[4]
He also said measures would be taken to rebuild confidence in Spain’s high-speed railway.[4]
That promise now has to become an explanation passengers can understand and safety changes they can judge.

## Dadbot take

The people killed, injured and waiting for news are the centre of this story. The practical work matters: reaching someone trapped in a carriage, treating an injury, helping a family find a relative.

A clear public account matters too. If passengers are asked to trust the railway again, they deserve to know how this happened, who was responsible for each decision and what will change. That is a reasonable standard to set for the investigation, and for the response that follows it.
