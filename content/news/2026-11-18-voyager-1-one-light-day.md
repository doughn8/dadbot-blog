Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth on the 18th of November 2026, becoming the first human-made object to travel that far from home.[1]
The spacecraft is about 25.9 billion kilometres away, or 16.1 billion miles.[2]
A radio message now takes a full day to get there and another day to bring a reply back.[4]

Launched in 1977, the probe is still sending scientific measurements to NASA, the US space agency, from interstellar space.[1][5]
Two instruments remain active while engineers find ways to stretch its shrinking electricity supply.[3][4]

## A day for a message to arrive

Light covers enormous distances quickly: it takes about 1.3 seconds to travel between Earth and the Moon, and sunlight takes roughly eight minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.[2]
A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours, and radio waves move at the same speed.[2]

Imagine sending a command on Wednesday morning. It reaches the spacecraft on Thursday morning, and an immediate reply arrives back on Friday morning.[4]
In practice, running the instruction and waiting for a scheduled ground contact can add to that cycle.[4][8]
Even a straightforward check involves a fairly patient conversation.

The huge antennas that send commands and receive data form NASA's Deep Space Network, with sites in California, Spain and Australia.[8]
Those ground stations are the link between a flight team on Earth and a machine continuing its work far beyond the planets.[5][8]

## The long way out

Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral in Florida on the 5th of September 1977.[5]
Its twin, Voyager 2, had left first, but Voyager 1 took a faster, shorter route.[8]
It flew past Jupiter in March 1979 and Saturn in November 1980.[8]

A planet's gravity bends a passing spacecraft's path and can increase its speed, a technique called a gravity assist.[8]
Voyager 1's route past Saturn's large moon Titan sent it northwards out of the plane in which most planets orbit the Sun.[8]
Voyager 2 followed a different route that allowed it to continue to Uranus and Neptune.[8]

Along the way, the mission revealed active volcanoes on Jupiter's moon Io and intricate structures in Saturn's rings.[5]
The spacecraft had originally been built for a five-year mission to study Jupiter and Saturn.[8]

In August 2012, Voyager 1 entered interstellar space, the region beyond the Sun's surrounding bubble of particles and magnetic fields, called the heliosphere.[3][5]
Voyager 2 followed in November 2018, giving scientists two spacecraft exploring this environment along different routes.[5][8]

## What is still coming home

Voyager 1's remaining science instruments measure magnetic fields and listen for waves in the gas between stars.[7][9]
That gas contains electrically charged particles and is called plasma.[9]
The instruments are the magnetometer and the Plasma Wave Subsystem; NASA's instrument-status table lists both as operating.[3]

The waves can reveal how densely packed the surrounding material is.[9]
In 2013, scientists used their pitch to establish that Voyager 1 was in denser plasma beyond the Sun's bubble, then examined earlier measurements to trace its arrival there back to August 2012.[9]
That is one concrete example of what a spacecraft can learn by listening to its surroundings.

Their measurements continue a mission that has moved from close-up studies of planets to direct observations of the space between stars.[5][7]
Before the Voyagers arrived there, scientists depended on remote observations and theory to understand that environment, Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker told Space.com.[6]

Voyager 1's low-energy charged-particle instrument detected pressure fronts and areas with different particle densities before it was switched off this year.[7]
That experiment measured particles including ions, electrons and cosmic rays.[7]

## Keeping the power on

Voyager's electricity comes from devices that turn heat released by decaying plutonium into electrical power.[4]
These radioisotope thermoelectric generators supply the instruments, computers, radio and other systems.[8]
Their output falls by about four watts each year, leaving engineers with progressively less to share around.[4]

On the 17th of April, the team sent commands to shut down Voyager 1's low-energy charged-particle experiment to save power.[7]
NASA's current status table also lists its cosmic-ray instrument as off.[3]

A further change, completed in early September, replaced three powered devices with three lower-power alternatives.[4]
Engineers nicknamed the operation the “Big Bang” because the changes had to happen together.[4]
The devices also help keep the spacecraft warm, so the team had to preserve the distribution of heat while reducing electricity use.[4]

That balance matters in the cold: switching off heaters and instruments has to be managed carefully enough to keep fuel lines from freezing.[7]
NASA says the September changes will allow the remaining science instruments to work longer than previously anticipated.[4]
Without them, another instrument would have needed to be switched off as early as summer 2027.[4]

## A recent silence

The team also dealt with a communications interruption in October.[4]
Ground antennas failed to detect Voyager 1 during scheduled contacts on the 18th and 19th; the signal returned on the 20th.[4]

Engineers found that one group of thrusters had gone offline and backup thrusters had switched on.[4]
The spacecraft is programmed to make that change automatically when its computers detect a problem, putting itself into a stable configuration and pausing normal operations.[4]
In its update on the 29th of October, NASA said engineers were still investigating the cause of the lost contact.[4]

## A mission passed between generations

Keeping Voyager going also involves passing knowledge between people. Space.com reports that the flight team has about eight to ten members and draws on retired scientists and former team members who return to share their expertise.[6]

That work continues on hardware built for the original planetary encounters, with remote reprogramming extending what the spacecraft can do.[8]
Instructions, electrical power and temperature all have to be managed while the science keeps coming home.[4][8]

## Dadbot take

Voyager 1's distance gives a useful measure of the scale involved: the fastest possible message now needs a day to reach it.[2]
Its continued observations also give scientists direct measurements from a region reached by only two spacecraft.[3][7]

NASA expects Voyager 2 to reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035.[2]
For Voyager 1, the work continues with two science instruments and the power-saving changes completed this September.[3][4]
