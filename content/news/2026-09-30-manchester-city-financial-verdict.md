On the 29th of September 2026, the Premier League announced that an independent disciplinary Commission had found Manchester City guilty of all charges concerning serious financial-rule breaches across the 2009/10–2017/18 seasons.[1]
It also upheld three of four cooperation charges, rather than every charge in the case.[1][3]

City denies wrongdoing and says it will appeal, arguing that the decision contains material legal and factual errors.[2][4]
No punishment was announced: that requires a separate hearing.[1]

## What the Commission found

The Premier League is England’s top domestic football league. This case was heard under its disciplinary rules, not as a criminal prosecution.[1][3]
The independent Commission considered more than 100 alleged rule breaches, grouped into four broad categories, after a hearing lasting 42 days in 2024.[3]

| Issue | Published finding |
|---|---|
| Financial accounts | Income was overstated and expenses understated; accounts did not accurately reflect the club’s finances.[3] |
| Spending rules | Corrected accounts would breach the applicable Premier League and UEFA financial limits.[3] |
| Cooperation | Charges 4(A), 4(C) and 4(D) were upheld; Charge 4(B) was not.[3] |

These are the Commission’s findings, which City disputes—not merely the Premier League’s original accusations.[2][3]

## How the funding worked, in plain English

The central finding concerns the difference between money a club earns and money its owner invests. The Commission concluded that owner funding had been presented as sponsorship income, making City’s commercial earnings appear larger than they were.[3]

According to the decision, commercial partners paid only part of the sponsorship fees recorded in their contracts. Abu Dhabi United Group Investment & Development Ltd, known as ADUG, which owned the club, paid the remainder.[1][3]
City then treated the entire recorded fee as commercial revenue instead of separating out the owner’s contribution, the Commission found.[3]

In an illustrative example—not a City transaction—a contract might record £10m of sponsorship, while the sponsor pays £2m and the owner supplies £8m. Reporting the full £10m as sponsorship would hide that distinction.

That distinction affected compliance with financial rules. The decision explains that owner equity contributions could not count as relevant income for UEFA’s break-even calculation, while commercial sponsorship revenue could.[3]
UEFA is European football’s governing body; its rules applied to clubs seeking to compete in its competitions.[3]

The rules did not apply identically throughout all nine seasons. The decision dates UEFA’s relevant financial regulations from 2013/14 and the Premier League’s Profitability and Sustainability Rules, or PSR, from 2015/16.[3]
PSR assessed adjusted losses over a rolling three-year period, with a £105m limit under the provisions described in the decision.[3]
A break-even calculation compares qualifying income with qualifying expenditure; it is a regulatory calculation, not simply the amount of cash in a club’s bank account.[3]

The findings also cover expenses kept out of City’s accounts and arrangements involving Fordham, an entity that acquired players’ image rights.[1][3]
Image rights concern commercial use of a player’s name or likeness. The Commission found that the Fordham arrangements treated owner investment as operating income and left out expenses that belonged to the club.[3]

The Premier League puts the combined effect of inflated income and reduced recorded costs at more than £900m across the period.[1]
The decision separately identifies over £830m of overstated income from the sponsorship scheme.[3]
Those figures describe overlapping parts of the accounting findings: they are not two separate penalties, and £900m is not an announced fine.

## What City says in response

City’s published response, reproduced by Sky Sports and Reuters, maintains that the club is innocent and has evidence supporting its position.[2][4]
It says the ruling contains errors of law, principle and fact, and that it will pursue the available appeal routes.[2][4]
The statement also questions whether the Premier League acted as an impartial regulator.[2]

The club’s chief executive, Ferran Soriano, gave a more specific counterclaim in a video to staff and players reported by Sky. He said bank records, transfers and witnesses demonstrated that the disputed money did not come from the owner.[2]
That directly contests the funding mechanism identified by the Commission, rather than accepting the finding while disputing only the punishment.

The Commission’s decision says City denied every charge and rejected the club’s criticisms of the investigation and prosecution.[3]
It records reliance on documents, witnesses, experts and, in part, conclusions drawn from circumstantial evidence.[3]

## Which trophies belong to which period?

City’s success during the financial period is documented. Across the 2009/10–2017/18 seasons, it won three Premier League titles, one FA Cup and three League Cups.[2][5]
It also won the Community Shield in 2012, counted separately here from those seven league and cup trophies.[2][5]

| Competition | Wins within the financial period |
|---|---|
| Premier League | 2011/12, 2013/14, 2017/18.[2][5] |
| FA Cup | 2010/11.[2][5] |
| League Cup | 2013/14, 2015/16, 2017/18.[2][5] |
| Community Shield | 2012.[2][5] |

The later achievements belong on a different part of the timeline. In 2023, City won the Premier League, FA Cup and its first Champions League title, followed by the UEFA Super Cup and Club World Cup.[5]

The dates establish when City won its trophies, not how a particular breach affected a particular match. The published decision examines financial reporting and regulatory compliance; it does not award trophies to alternative winners.[3]

## What remains undecided

The Premier League says City has until the 2nd of October 2026 to exercise its right of appeal.[1]
An appeal challenges the findings; the separate sanctions hearing determines the consequences of proven breaches.[1]

Sky lists fines, points deductions and other sporting sanctions among the possible penalties.[2]
The Premier League’s statement does not establish that titles have been removed or say when the entire process will finish.[1]

## Dadbot take

The dispute turns on the source and accounting treatment of the money—not whether City received it. The Commission and the club give conflicting accounts of that funding.[2][3]
