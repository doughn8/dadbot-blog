On the 19th of November 2026, the International Court of Justice, the United Nations’ top court, ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State during a military crackdown in 2017.[1][2]
It was the first time the court found that a state had committed genocide.[3]

In Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, Rahama Khatun watched the judges on her phone from the shelter where she lives with her four youngest children.[4]
Her husband, brother and brother-in-law were among ten Rohingya men killed in the village of Inn Din in 2017, according to a Reuters investigation.[4]
She fled Inn Din while pregnant, with her eight children.[4]
The family spent several days on a sandbank near the border before a boatman took them across to Bangladesh, Reuters reports.[4]

## Why The Gambia brought the case

The Gambia filed the case in November 2019 under the Genocide Convention, an international treaty that requires countries to prevent and punish genocide.[2]
The Gambia had the backing of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, a group of 57 countries.[2]
The treaty allows one country that has joined it to bring a dispute about another member’s responsibility before the court.[2]
When Myanmar challenged the court’s authority to hear the case, the judges ruled in 2022 that all countries bound by the treaty have a shared interest in seeing it upheld, so The Gambia could bring the case.[2]
The court later held public hearings on the substance of the case in January 2026, hearing witnesses from both sides.[2]

The court settles disputes between states, so the ruling sets out what Myanmar as a country is responsible for and what it must do.[1][2]
Any prosecution of someone suspected of taking part would deal with that person’s own conduct.[1][2]
The court had already ordered Myanmar to protect the Rohingya and preserve evidence while the case was under way; in this judgment, it found Myanmar had breached the order to preserve evidence.[1][2]

## How the judges reached the genocide finding

The judges had to establish what Myanmar’s forces intended: whether they meant to destroy the Rohingya as a group, in whole or in part.[1]
When intent has to be worked out from the circumstances, the court’s test is whether it is the only reasonable conclusion the evidence supports.[1]
The court requires evidence that fully convinces it that the acts alleged as genocide have been established.[1]
The judges checked whether other credible evidence backed up the UN investigators’ reports.[1]
They used satellite images to help establish which villages had burned or been destroyed.[1]

Myanmar argued its military operation was a response to attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army, an armed group.[1]
The court accepted that the group attacked security posts in August 2017 and that the military operations followed in some of the same areas.[1]
It found that Myanmar’s forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians and committed widespread sexual violence, and that Rohingya villages and farmland were deliberately destroyed.[1]
The court described an organised effort to replace some of those places with permanent structures and to resettle other communities on land where Rohingya families had lived.[1]
The judges found no evidence that the forces had distinguished civilians from suspected fighters during the crackdown.[1]
Nine judges to six found Myanmar had committed genocide; the majority concluded that the evidence taken together pointed to an intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya group, namely the population in northern Rakhine State.[1]

## What Myanmar must do

The court ordered Myanmar to help Rohingya displaced by its actions return safely, voluntarily and with dignity, and to reunite families.[1]
That applies to people displaced within Myanmar as well as those who fled abroad.[1]
The judgment also orders Myanmar to return or replace lost or damaged property, help find people who went missing, and provide physical and psychological rehabilitation to people harmed.[1]
For families with relatives still missing, the order goes beyond a search: it calls for help identifying those who were killed and burying them according to Rohingya traditions.[1]
The judges included protection from persecution and discrimination, including in access to citizenship and freedom of movement.[1]

On accountability, Myanmar must take immediate, effective steps to investigate people reasonably suspected of acts covered by the treaty during the 2017 operations, then prosecute and punish them where warranted.[1]
It must also bring its laws into line with the treaty and take steps to prevent similar violations.[1]
The Gambia brought the case, but the people entitled to the remedies are the Rohingya, individually and as a group.[1]

## Myanmar’s response and what happens next

On the 20th of November, government spokeswoman Khine Khine Soe called the decision unfair, pointing to the split among the judges.[5]
She said the government would take time to study the judgment and the judges’ opinions.[5]
Myanmar had denied committing genocide, and the spokeswoman gave no response to the specific orders on return, compensation or prosecution in the remarks reported by AP.[5]
The ruling on Myanmar’s responsibility is binding and cannot be appealed.[3]

The judgment says Myanmar owes compensation for harm that measures like returning property cannot put right.[1]
The judges gave Myanmar two years from the judgment to report on what it has done to repair the harm and to set out compensation proposals where that harm cannot be put right by restoring what was lost.[1]
The court will hear from both countries and decide the amount and form of compensation or other remedies in a later phase.[1]
The two-year deadline is for the report; the judgment sets no date for families to go home.[1]

## Dadbot take

AP reports that about 1.2 million Rohingya refugees live in Bangladesh today, mostly in crowded camps around Cox’s Bazar.[6]
Hundreds gathered around a big screen there to watch the ruling, and refugees told AP that getting home safely was what they wanted to see happen next.[6]
Reuters reports that fighting continues in Rakhine between Myanmar’s military and the Arakan Army, a separate armed group.[4]
Khatun told Reuters she wants to go home and live there as a citizen with equal rights.[4]
The ruling pairs the requirement to make return safe with protections that matter after the journey: freedom to move and access to citizenship without discrimination.[1]
