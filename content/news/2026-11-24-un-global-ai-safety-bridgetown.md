António Guterres, the United Nations secretary-general, and Mia Mottley, the prime minister of Barbados, asked countries to work out a common approach to artificial intelligence (AI) safety at the Bridgetown summit in Barbados on the 23rd of November 2026.[1][3]
They want governments to develop a framework within the next year, with independent oversight.[2][3]

Two of the ideas discussed at the summit give that proposal a practical shape: testing AI in ways that let different countries compare results, and letting them warn one another when something goes wrong.[1][5]
For a government buying software built abroad, those are two useful things to be able to do.

## What happened

The UN and the Barbadian government co-hosted the summit, which brought together government officials, technology companies, scientists and civil society groups.[1]
Guterres and Mottley closed it by issuing the Bridgetown Call to Action on AI Safety for Humanity.[1]
It asks UN member states to coordinate work already under way and suggests starting discussions with the jobs countries could do together.[2]

Guterres told reporters that protections were fragmented and described a common foundation that could include basic requirements for companies to explain what their systems do, shared safeguards and independent checks on advanced models.[6]
At the opening, he said that countries should be able to compare evaluations, use common terms and learn from serious incidents while still making their own national decisions.[5]
The joint text places that sort of cooperation alongside domestic and regional rules.[2]

## How countries could compare safety tests

The text records calls for testing before and after an AI system goes into use, including checks in local languages by people outside the developer.[2]
Guterres argued for tests whose results can be compared across borders and for giving each country the means to check whether a system works safely where it is used.[5]
The Call also records a suggestion to make safety part of the purchase itself, including giving buyers a way to switch a system off.[2]

Suppose two public agencies are looking at the same AI assistant to help people find government services. One team checks whether the assistant gives the right advice about which office to call. The other checks how often people get a useful answer in a language the system handles less well. Both teams might come back with a percentage. To compare them, a reader would need to know what each team counted as a failure.

A shared test could have the two teams record the version of the software, the kinds of questions they asked and what counted as a serious error. They could then compare whether the same failure showed up in both places. Each could still test the words and situations people actually use locally. A public-service assistant has a fairly basic job: sending someone to the right office is a better test than making the directions sound confident.

## How an alert could travel

The Call records support for international incident reporting and notification, with arrangements for responding to routine problems and emergencies.[2]

Imagine an update to that assistant starts sending some people to the wrong office for time-sensitive help. One agency spots the pattern after staff receive complaints. A warning sent to other agencies using the software could tell them which version was involved, what people were told and which questions seemed to trigger the bad advice. A team elsewhere could check its own service and decide whether to put those questions back in the hands of staff while it investigates.

That example leaves a practical choice for any future framework: which incidents should be reported quickly across borders, and which can be handled locally? The Call also records appeals for more accountability for the companies that build AI tools and the organisations that put them into use.[2]
That matters for an alert: the organisation that spots the problem might be a customer, while the company able to fix the update is somewhere else.

## Why local testing matters

The joint text says every country should be able to build, test and use AI on its own terms, and calls for a voice for every region in setting standards and designing tests.[2]
At the summit, Guterres pointed to research from an international scientific panel on AI about the value of testing a system in the languages people use, according to UN News.[6]
Mottley called attention to the need for people with the right skills, infrastructure and software that reflects local priorities, Barbados Today reported.[4]

The summit’s closing statement said a proposed fund for helping countries build that capacity was seeking about US$3 billion in initial commitments.[1]
The Call asks governments, businesses, financial institutions and donors in a position to help to invest in areas where that capacity is limited.[2]
The same text lists possible ways to put that support to work, including local datasets, trained staff and computing equipment that countries could share.[2]

Mottley gave a regional example of how international and domestic work could run in parallel. Barbados Today reported that she was prepared to bring in a Barbadian regulatory framework if an AI commission set up by the Caribbean Community, a group of countries in the region, had not delivered its report by February.[4]
She also expressed support for an international treaty, according to the same report.[4]

## Who would have the power to act?

The Call asks for independent oversight and leaves its design to future discussions.[2]
Governments would need to decide who checks the results, what information companies must share and whether the body handling a warning could require action or would pass the problem to national authorities.

## Dadbot take

The summit put two practical tasks on the table: making tests useful beyond the laboratory that ran them and getting warnings to people who can respond.[2][5]
For an agency that buys a tool made elsewhere, the distinction to watch is between being told there is a problem and having the authority to do something about it. For the person using the service, that might be the difference between getting sent somewhere useful and spending the morning on hold.
