A New Mexico judge ordered Meta, Facebook’s parent company, to pay $25 billion on the 21st of October 2026 after a jury found that it repeatedly misled the public about privacy and other practices.[1]
Meta plans to appeal.[5]
The judgment is enormous; the amount New Mexico eventually receives remains an open question.

The useful comparison is not just with other fines. It is with the deal other states accepted, the promises covered by that deal and the money Meta has actually paid. Those figures describe rather different things.

## What happened

Judge Francis J. Mathew, sitting in New Mexico’s state District Court in Santa Fe, imposed the penalty under the state’s Unfair Practices Act, its consumer-protection law.[1]
A jury had already decided liability; the judge then determined the financial penalty.[1]

The jury found 26 public representations misleading, with the court recording nearly 44 million violations.[1]
That does not mean 44 million different people: the counts reflected each statement’s expected audience, using either Facebook’s New Mexico users or the state’s population.[1]
The same person could therefore count towards more than one statement.[1]

The judge assessed roughly $219.5 billion under the law’s maximum of $5,000 per violation, then reduced the penalty to $25 billion.[1]
His order also sets 15% annual interest until payment.[1]
That is the court’s current order, not a forecast of the final bill after appeals.

## Why this matters

The case grew out of Cambridge Analytica, the political consultancy that obtained Facebook users’ information through a third-party app for voter profiling.[4]
But the court’s findings reach beyond that scandal, covering Facebook’s statements about data control, misinformation, hate speech and equal enforcement of its rules.[1]

One concrete example is the difference between choosing who sees a post and controlling what an outside app can collect. The court found Facebook’s privacy settings did not give users meaningful control in several respects, and that developers could obtain information about an app user’s friends without those friends knowing.[1]

The court also found Facebook failed to carry out the comprehensive investigation it promised after the scandal, and relied on developers’ assurances about deleting data rather than independently checking deletion.[1]
A reassuring settings menu is not much comfort if the important decisions happen somewhere else.

## Which states settled?

New Mexico was **not the only state outside the wider agreement**: Meta’s updated participant list includes 48 states, plus Washington DC, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands and Puerto Rico; New Mexico and Florida are absent.[3]
That is 52 attorneys general, not 52 states.[3]

There is another list inside the paperwork. The August agreement includes a separate **$459.3 million Cambridge Analytica settlement**, releasing participating jurisdictions’ relevant privacy claims alongside the wider teen-safety deal.[2]
The filed schedule for that particular pot lists **46 states and two territories**.[2]
The agreement names New Mexico’s complaint when defining the claims it covers, but New Mexico is not on that recipient list.[2]
In plain English, other jurisdictions accepted money to close off comparable claims; New Mexico pursued its own case.

These are different recipient lists, not competing answers to one question. Treating the whole agreement as a privacy fine, or the Cambridge payment as the entire child-safety settlement, would muddle the comparison.

## How much has Meta actually paid?

There is a solid starting figure: **Facebook paid the $5 billion Federal Trade Commission settlement in April 2020**, according to its financial results.[6]
The Federal Trade Commission is the US consumer-protection regulator; that payment followed the earlier privacy enforcement case.[4]

The newer figures need different labels:

- **$459.3 million:** the Cambridge settlement obligation in the filed August agreement.[2]
Its payment clause requires payment within 30 days of the agreement’s effective date.[2]
- **Approximately $18 billion:** Meta’s description of the wider agreement, spread over ten years.[3]
It says approximately $12.7 billion is allocated for payment over the decade, while roughly $5.3 billion depends on conditions involving YouTube and TikTok.[3]
- **$725 million:** the separate private Cambridge Analytica class-action settlement reported by The Record, agreed in 2022.[5]
- **$25 billion:** the New Mexico civil judgment, with the interest ordered separately.[1]

A payment deadline is not a bank receipt. The retrieved settlement documents do not establish a complete cash total received so far, and the decade-long headline amount plainly cannot be treated as already paid. Nor does an agreement to fund a private settlement establish how much claimants have received.

Adding every headline together would produce an impressive number and a rather unhelpful answer.

## Will Meta pay the full $25 billion?

It is too early to call that likely. It would be equally rash to assume Meta will simply escape the judgment.

Meta’s chief legal officer, C.J. Mahoney, says the ruling makes doing business in New Mexico almost impossible and characterises the lawsuit as an attempt to tax speech; the company says it will appeal.[5]
Reuters reports that Meta has argued a multibillion-dollar penalty could breach constitutional protections against excessive fines and unfair legal treatment, and that New Mexico did not prove consumers were actually misled.[4]

The court’s reasoning points the other way: it emphasises repeated, intentional conduct over many years, senior leadership’s involvement and the number of violations.[1]
It also says Meta received notice and a full opportunity to defend itself at trial.[1]

The dispute is therefore about both the underlying findings and whether the penalty is legally justified, not simply whether $25 billion sounds enormous. Daryl Lim, a law professor quoted by Reuters, cautions that the size alone does not mean the penalty will be overturned.[4]

An appeal asks a higher court to review the decision. Until that process produces an outcome, there is no sound basis here for putting a percentage on full payment or promising New Mexico a particular collection date.

## What is still unclear

The judge has reserved decisions on further orders requiring changes in conduct.[1]
That leaves a practical question alongside the financial one: what changes will the court require Facebook to make? The exact cash received under the August settlement is also unverified here, rather than established as unpaid.

## Dadbot take

Taking the case to trial has produced a much bigger judgment than accepting a share of the settlement. That deserves attention. But the worthwhile test is whether it leads to money collected and promises that can actually be enforced, not who has the largest number on a press release.

A court order is serious. It is also not a cheque.
