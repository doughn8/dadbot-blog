The Trump administration proposed new US tariffs on goods from 14 trading partners on the 25th of November 2026, including China, the European Union and Vietnam.[1][3]
The Office of the US Trade Representative, which handles US trade negotiations and enforcement, says government policies in those economies have helped sustain more manufacturing capacity than demand would normally support.[1][4]

For a US business waiting on a shipment, the proposal raises a more immediate question: how much extra might it owe? The EU notice sets out a proposed 15% rate that takes ordinary customs duties and existing tariffs under the same trade law into account.[2]

## Who is on the list?

The proposal covers these 14 trading partners:[1]

- **Asia:** Bangladesh, Cambodia, China, India, Indonesia, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam.[1]
- **Europe:** the European Union and Switzerland.[1]

The US trade office opened 16 investigations in March 2026 and has yet to make determinations on Mexico and Norway.[1]
The investigations used Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a US law that allows the government to respond to foreign practices it finds are unfair and burden US commerce.[1]
The November announcement sets out findings for each of the 14 and seeks comments on the tariffs proposed in response.[1][2]

## Why Washington says it is acting

Think of a factory with enough machinery to make twice as many goods as it can sell at home. It could find customers overseas, reduce output or leave some machines idle. Washington’s concern is that policies such as cheap financing or incentives to expand can keep factories producing or building capacity for longer than demand would otherwise justify.[1][4]
That persistent gap is what US officials call *structural excess capacity*.[4]

The findings name different practices in different economies. In Bangladesh, for example, the trade office cited production incentives and plans to expand manufacturing, singling out ready-made clothes as a sector of concern.[1]
US officials say those practices cut into opportunities for US producers both in Bangladesh and in other markets where they sell.[1]

In the EU, US officials pointed to measures such as export credit and favourable loans aimed at manufacturers.[1]
They identified cars, chemicals and pharmaceuticals as industries where they say the effects are especially pronounced.[1]

The European Commission, the EU’s executive arm, disputes the US finding about Europe.[3]
Its spokesman, Olof Gill, said Europe was itself exposed to global overcapacity and that the Commission expected Washington to honour the 2025 agreement, including how new tariffs would combine with existing duties.[3]

## What 15% would mean for an EU shipment

A tariff is a tax on imports. The party legally responsible for the bill is the *importer of record*, even when a customs broker handles the declaration.[5]
For the EU, the trade office proposes counting the ordinary tariff and any existing Section 301 duty on a covered product towards the headline 15% rate.[2][4]
The notice calls the ordinary tariff used in this calculation the most-favoured-nation rate, or MFN.[2][4]

Take a shipment with a customs value of $10,000 and an ordinary duty of 5%. To keep the maths simple, assume the product is covered by the proposal and there are no other duties. The first $500 comes from the ordinary duty. Under the 15% proposal, the new charge would be the remaining 10 percentage points, or $1,000, bringing those duties to $1,500.[2][4]

The same notice gives Washington another option: a rate of up to 20%, with the same treatment of ordinary and existing Section 301 duties, if US officials judge ongoing consultations and existing commitments unsatisfactory.[2]

## How a product can be exempt

Goods already subject to certain separate US tariffs on steel, aluminium and passenger vehicles are among the proposed EU exemptions.[2]
The notice also lists items such as books and some raw materials, including materials the US trade office says could become unavailable domestically if hit with the proposed duties.[2]
An exemption leaves the importer to account for whatever other duties already apply to the shipment.[2]

Some exemptions come down to the product code in the annex, the list attached to the notice.[2]
That list follows the US tariff schedule, the catalogue customs uses to classify imports, and includes entries that cover only part of what a particular code usually describes.[2]
The proposed tariff treatment therefore depends on the description and any scope limits beside the code in the notice for that trading partner.[2][4]

## China and Vietnam have different terms

For China, US trade officials propose up to 20% for goods outside two lists of exceptions, taking account of a separate Section 301 tariff tied to forced labour.[1][4]
One list would face up to 10%, while the other would be exempt from the new action.[1][4]
A senior US official told reporters that the new charge would be added to first-term Trump tariffs on Chinese goods that already face those duties.[3][4]

For Vietnam, Washington proposes considering up to 35%, taking existing Section 301 duties into account, with exemptions for certain goods.[1]
The trade office also puts forward a 20% rate if Vietnam reaches an agreement that addresses the US findings.[1]

Japan and South Korea are among the partners for which the US proposes a rate of up to 15% that takes ordinary duties and existing Section 301 tariffs into account, as in the EU example.[1][4]
In some of these notices, Washington is also considering a higher alternative if it judges talks and existing commitments unsatisfactory.[4]

## What happens next

Written comments and requests to speak at the hearings are due by the 5th of January 2027.[1][2]
Anyone asking to speak must also send a summary of their testimony by that deadline.[2]
The hearings are scheduled to begin on the 25th of January at the US International Trade Commission in Washington, DC.[2]

There was already a round of public input during the investigations. The trade office says it heard from nearly 140 witnesses and received 1,070 comments across all 16 inquiries.[1]
This time, the EU notice asks which products to include, which to leave out and how any new charges should account for duties already in place.[2]
