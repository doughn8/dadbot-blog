Hundreds of French high schools were disrupted on Monday the 5th of October as pupils continued protests over staffing, crowded classes and the conditions in which they study.[2][8]
Education Minister Édouard Geffray said that 589 establishments had been affected that day, according to an afternoon report by Europe 1.[8]
That is a disruption count, not a count of schools closed.

Pupil organisations have called for another day of action on the 6th of October, while Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has asked ministers for decisions before the end of the month.[4][6]
The argument is about daily school life, but clashes, injuries and closures have added a separate dispute over safety and policing.[2]

## What happened

The movement centres on *lycées*: the schools pupils generally attend between the ages of 15 and 18, before entering higher education or work.[1]
AFP traces an early protest to Saint-Exupéry high school in Créteil, a suburb south-east of Paris, on the 17th of September.[1]
Pupils joined teachers who were seeking more staff and resources.[1]

Videos spread through social media, including TikTok, helping the protests reach other schools.[1]
Reports from other schools describe similar grievances, but the problems and demands are not identical everywhere.[3][4]

At the school where that early action began, authorities agreed to provide additional staff, including a nurse and a deputy head, and normal school activity resumed, AFP reported.[1]
Elsewhere, the movement continued.[2]

## Why this matters

Pupils’ complaints concern lessons they are missing and the conditions in which they are expected to learn, rather than one new national rule.[3]
Teacher and parent representatives interviewed by Euronews also described difficulty replacing absent teachers and concern about overcrowding and classrooms that become too hot.[2]

The protests themselves can interrupt education too.[2]
Some schools have been closed wholly or partly because authorities judged that pupils and staff could not be received safely.[2]
The authorities are dealing with both the education complaints and immediate risks around school sites.[2][6]

## What we know so far

- The movement began with education grievances and has spread beyond the original school.[1]
- Pupil representatives have condemned violence while calling for continued mobilisation; attacks and damage have nevertheless occurred around some demonstrations.[4][2]
- The government has announced work on pupils’ concerns, but its response includes future decisions and meetings, not a package of completed changes.[6]

## Why pupils are protesting

**Missing teachers and support staff.** Pupils interviewed for reporting by 20 Minutes and AFP complained of lessons without a teacher, including important subjects such as French.[3]
Their concern is not simply that a teacher takes time off: it is that nobody replaces them. A timetable can promise a lesson without the lesson actually happening.

**Crowded classes and long days.** The same reporting describes pupils objecting to overloaded timetables, including days running from eight in the morning until six in the evening, and classes they consider too large.[3]
These are reported examples, not a claim that every French teenager follows that schedule.

**The buildings themselves.** Pupils have described damaged ceilings, equipment that does not work and rooms that become excessively hot.[3]
Teachers interviewed by Euronews said adapting classrooms to heat was an existing concern, not something invented during the protests.[2]

**What happens after school.** Parcoursup is France’s online application system for most higher-education courses.[3]
Some pupils say it is stressful and insufficiently transparent; demands range from changes to how it works to its abolition.[3]
That is a different question from asking for more teachers, although both concern whether pupils can complete school and move into the course they want.

There is also frustration about being consulted without feeling heard.[4]
After talks on the 2nd of October, Geffray acknowledged problems with school democracy, according to specialist education publication L’Etudiant.[4]
In plain English, that means whether pupils’ representatives have a meaningful say in decisions affecting their school.

## What the government has promised

In a statement on the 4th of October, Lecornu set out five areas of work: ensuring lessons take place, improving buildings with regional authorities, organising school days, making Parcoursup more transparent and giving visible responses to pupils’ views.[10]

The reported instructions include prioritising long teacher absences and unfilled posts, with an initial assessment due on the 16th of October.[6]
Lecornu also called for a meeting with regional leaders on the 6th of October to identify urgent building needs and a timetable for dealing with them.[6]
That matters because regional authorities are responsible for lycée buildings; improving them is not solely a decision for the national education ministry.[3]

Ministers have been asked to present initial decisions, costs, funding and schedules before the end of October, BFMTV reported.[6]
These deadlines describe work requested by the prime minister. They do not establish that extra staff have arrived everywhere or buildings have already been repaired.

## Where they agree — and where they do not

The minister has recognised concerns raised by pupils, and teacher organisations say many match their own longstanding complaints.[4]
The disagreement is over whether the response is concrete and sufficient: pupil representatives told L’Etudiant that meetings had not produced the commitments they expected.[4]
The FCPE, a national parents’ organisation, has called for parents to demonstrate alongside pupils on the 6th of October.[5]

A separate political argument concerns La France insoumise, a left-wing party whose leader, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, supports the protests.[1]
Government figures have accused the party of encouraging the unrest; party officials deny organising it.[1][2]
Support and proven control of a movement are not the same thing.

## What is still unclear

In Lens, northern France, a 15-year-old pupil suffered a severe hand injury involving a police grenade on the 5th of October.[7][9]
The prosecutor said he could not yet establish whether the pupil picked up the grenade or it landed on him.[7]
The investigation has been assigned to the IGPN, the body that investigates police conduct.[7]

In Tours, a separate investigation concerns a pupil who lost sight in one eye; prosecutors said the injuries suggested a possible impact from a police rubber bullet.[2]
Those investigations have not settled responsibility. Neither dramatic footage nor an account of the wider protests resolves what happened in each case.

It is also too early to know how many pupils will join the next action, or whether the government’s coming decisions will satisfy their demands.

## The factual picture

The protests concern education conditions, while the response now also involves school safety and investigations into serious injuries.[2][6][7]
The announced response has not yet produced a settled outcome.

## Sources and caveats

This account reflects reporting checked on the 5th of October 2026. Disruption figures are dated, attributed snapshots. Sources describe both an early school protest and a later spread of mobilisation, so their start dates differ. Some reports share AFP material or official briefings; they are not separate proof of every event. The prime minister’s public statement was read directly; detailed instructions in his letter are reported through BFMTV. FCPE statements establish its own position. Injury investigations remain open.
