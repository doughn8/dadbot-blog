Democrats have won control of the House of Representatives and the Senate in the US midterm elections, according to the BBC's results tracker and CBS News' projections.[1][6]
Donald Trump remains president, but the incoming Congress will put both chambers that make federal law in opposition hands.[1][6]

If you live outside America, the useful question is what that changes in practice. New majorities can mean different decisions about money, trade and support for other countries. Winning seats is the start. Turning them into a different policy takes the next round of votes and negotiations.

## The results so far

The BBC and Guardian trackers showed the following position on the 4th of November 2026.[1][2]

### House of Representatives

- **Democrats:** 237 seats.
- **Republicans:** 139 seats.
- **Races called:** 376 of 435.
- **Seats needed for a majority:** 218.

### Senate

- **Democratic-aligned bloc:** 54 seats, including two independents.
- **Republicans:** 44 seats.
- **Races called:** 33 of the 35 contested seats.
- **Still unresolved:** Alaska and Mississippi.

The Senate total covers the whole incoming chamber, including seats not contested this year.[1][2]

A race call means the reporting organisation judges that the trailing candidate cannot catch up, which can happen before every ballot is counted.[2]
The remaining races will determine the size of the majorities.

## The seats behind the change

CBS reports Democratic gains in Texas, South Carolina, Kansas, Maine, Iowa, Ohio and North Carolina, alongside successful defences in Georgia, New Hampshire and Michigan.[6]

In Texas, James Talarico defeated Republican Ken Paxton; in Kansas, Adam Hamilton defeated incumbent Roger Marshall.[3]
Ballotpedia records Hamilton's win as Kansas's first election of a Democratic senator since 1932.[3]
In Ohio, former Democratic senator Sherrod Brown defeated Jon Husted in the special election for the remaining two years of the term originally won by JD Vance, now vice-president.[3]

## What changes in January?

The new congressional terms begin at noon on the 3rd of January 2027, under the Constitution's Twentieth Amendment.[7]
Until then, the existing Congress remains in place.[7]

Congress is America's national legislature, made up of the House and Senate.[1]
Both chambers must approve a bill before it goes to the president, who can sign it or use a veto to reject it.[8]
Overriding a regular veto requires two-thirds of members present and voting in each chamber, with enough members attending to conduct business.[8]

The Senate also approves senior presidential appointments, including judges, giving its new majority a separate influence over who takes important government jobs.[2]
Fox News congressional correspondent Chad Pergram argues that moderate Democrats who won Republican territory will constrain more ambitious progressive proposals.[5]

## Ukraine: more room for an aid debate

Reuters reports that Trump has largely halted direct American financial and military aid to Ukraine, despite support for assistance among lawmakers from both parties.[4]
Democratic control creates an opening for new aid proposals, with Republican backing needed to overcome a presidential veto.[4]

For Ukraine, the practical stakes include air defence during attacks on cities and energy infrastructure.[4]
Reuters warns that any increase in aid is unlikely to arrive quickly and would not solve the immediate shortage of American-made Patriot interceptors, missiles used to defend against incoming attacks.[4]

## Iran, Israel and energy

Reuters reports that the war with Iran has restricted Gulf oil and liquefied natural gas supplies, raising fuel costs internationally.[4]
It also reports that some Gulf governments see the result as another reason for Trump to seek a peace deal, citing two sources familiar with their thinking.[4]

In the Reuters report, Ebtesam al-Ketbi of the Emirates Policy Center describes two possible paths: a genuine negotiating opportunity, or renewed escalation if Iran reads the result as American weakness.[4]

For Israel, Reuters points to potentially stronger congressional scrutiny of military operations and pressure over American military aid and weapons sales.[4]
Those are concrete areas where a new majority could challenge the administration's approach.

## Trade: a different negotiating table

A tariff is a tax on imports.[4]
Reuters notes that Congress regulates trade and import duties, but existing laws have allowed Trump to impose tariffs on national-security or unfair-trade grounds.[4]
Attempts by Democrats to restrict those powers would probably face his veto, the report says.[4]

Analysts cited by Reuters suggest some Asian governments could become slower to fulfil investment promises made to avoid tariffs, while China might see a stronger negotiating position.[4]
The report also identifies possible breathing room for India's trade negotiations, where protecting farmers has been a sticking point.[4]

For a business agreeing an overseas order, the useful detail is the tariff actually applying when goods arrive.

## What remains unsettled?

Beyond the outstanding races, the political question is what the incoming Democrats will prioritise together. CBS reports that their Senate leader, Chuck Schumer, is promising accountability and investigations, while Kansas winner Hamilton wants the focus on practical cooperation rather than investigations or impeachment.[6]

The international consequences will take shape through specific proposals, votes and decisions by governments.
