US President Donald Trump suggested on the 19th of October 2026 that somebody else should negotiate for Ukraine, as Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy appealed to American lawmakers over delayed weapons and restrictions on military support.[1][3]
The argument now covers sanctions on Russia, satellite services and intelligence sharing, not just another bad exchange between two presidents.[1]

That raises a question: if America stops backing Ukraine, does peace become more likely? Possibly. But making one country more desperate for an agreement is not quite the same as persuading both countries to stop fighting.

## What happened

Trump waived new sanctions against Russia on the 16th of October, after Congress had passed them and he had signed the legislation in September.[2][7]
US Secretary of State Marco Rubio described a 60-day pause intended to give negotiations a chance, while also considering the measures' economic impact.[2][7]

The law gave Trump powers to impose tariffs (taxes on imported goods) of up to 100% on imports from major buyers of Russian oil and gas, including China and India.[7]
It also targeted senior Russian figures, financial institutions and ships used to get around existing embargoes.[7]
In other words, the pressure was meant to reach both Moscow and the trade helping to fund it.

Trump's explanation is that extra pressure could drive Russia away from the bargaining table.[6]
The decision follows his announcement of a Russian diesel agreement intended to ease fuel prices; the Guardian reported that commercial terms were still being negotiated.[4]
An announcement is not the same as fuel arriving at an American port.

Meanwhile, Zelenskyy told a bipartisan group of US lawmakers that the Pentagon, the American defence department, was delaying missile deliveries already paid for by partners.[1][3]
He also said Ukraine had missed advance warnings of Russian attacks; Trump and Rubio deny that Washington has stopped intelligence sharing.[1][2]

## Why this matters

This is not an abstract argument about diplomatic manners. Ukrainian officials said a Russian drone attack on Nova Vodolaha, in the Kharkiv region, killed six people, including two children, on the 19th of October.[2]
Zelenskyy says Ukraine has almost no American Patriot interceptors left: missiles used by its air-defence systems to shoot down incoming threats.[1]

Some weapons at issue are being purchased by European partners through PURL, a programme for buying American equipment for Ukraine.[3]
That matters because paying for equipment and getting permission for it to arrive are different problems. More European money would not automatically remove an American delivery or approval bottleneck.

The same distinction applies to negotiating pressure. Reducing support could make Ukraine more willing to accept terms it previously rejected. But if Russia thinks Ukraine's position will keep deteriorating, it could see an advantage in waiting instead. Those are competing possibilities, not a reliable forecast.

## What we know so far

- **New sanctions have been paused, not every sanction abolished.** The reported waiver concerns measures in recently passed legislation.[7]
- **Support restrictions are contested.** Zelenskyy reports satellite speed limits affecting drone operations; Trump denies ordering restrictions.[1]
- **Responsibility remains unresolved.** AP reports conflicting explanations about Starshield, SpaceX's military satellite service, and its relationship with Ukraine's access.[1]
- **Diplomacy has not stopped.** Reuters reported planned talks in Norway this week, citing people familiar with the matter, with Zelenskyy expected to attend.[3]

Rubio also acknowledged limited progress and said a deal would depend on both sides making concessions.[2]
That is a more useful starting point than treating a meeting announcement as a peace agreement.

## What is still unclear

There is no settled public account of who imposed which satellite restrictions, or which intelligence streams Ukraine is receiving.[1][2]
Zelenskyy's allegations about delayed missiles also need to be distinguished from an independently verified inventory or delivery schedule.[1][3]
A complete American cutoff is not established by these reports.

Nor do the reports establish a mutually accepted settlement, agreed security guarantees or a way to enforce them.[2][3]
Security guarantees mean commitments about who would help Ukraine, and how, if an agreement were broken. Without specifics, the phrase can sound considerably more reassuring than it is.

## How the right is framing it

The right-leaning New York Post leads with Trump's defence of sanctions relief as a route to peace and cheaper fuel, alongside his criticism of Ukrainian refinery attacks.[6]
The strongest version of that argument is practical: diplomacy needs incentives as well as punishment, and keeping Russia talking might achieve more than another restriction.

But there is no single Republican position. The BBC reports that Republican senator Ted Cruz called the waiver a mistake, while fellow Republican Thom Tillis strongly criticised it.[7]
Support for Ukraine does not fit neatly into a two-column party chart.[7]

## How the left is framing it

The left-leaning Guardian puts the sanctions decision alongside the increasingly hostile exchanges and continued attacks on energy infrastructure.[4]
Its briefing also includes Trump's stated negotiating rationale rather than leaving it out.[4]

That emphasis directs attention towards the cost to Ukraine of reducing pressure on Moscow. It does not establish that every concession to Russia must fail; it asks what Russia is offering in return.

## Where both sides overlap

Both reports describe sanctions relief and carry Trump's explanation that he wants to preserve negotiations.[4][6]
The disagreement is not whether talks exist, but whether these decisions improve their chances of producing something worthwhile.

There is also a concrete cross-party response: Democratic senator Richard Blumenthal and Republican senator Thom Tillis organised the call with Zelenskyy.[1]

## Dadbot read

The question is worth asking. More weapons are not, on their own, a peace plan. Neither is switching them off.

Less American backing might produce a faster Ukrainian signature. A durable peace would also need Russia to prefer the agreement to further fighting, and Ukraine to have a credible reason to trust what follows. The useful question is not just whether pressure brings somebody to the table, but what keeps the deal standing after everyone leaves.
