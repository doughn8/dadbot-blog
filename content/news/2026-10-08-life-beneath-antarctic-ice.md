Scientists have found active, marine-associated microbes around Blood Falls, the red outflow at the edge of Taylor Glacier in East Antarctica. Their study, published in *Nature Geoscience* on the 3rd of August 2026, adds genetic evidence to the idea that seawater once entered the valley and later became trapped beneath ice.[1]

Elsewhere, underwater surveys have recorded corals, sponges and other animals on seabed previously covered by floating Antarctic ice. These are separate discoveries in different environments: microscopic life around a glacier's outlet, and marine animals living beneath an ice shelf.[4][6]

## What happened at Blood Falls

Blood Falls lies in the McMurdo Dry Valleys, a cold, dry region of Antarctica. Its water comes from an iron-rich brine system associated with Taylor Glacier; brine means very salty water.[2]
When the water emerges and meets the air, the iron reacts with oxygen and produces the red colour.[3]

Earlier work had identified bacteria associated with the brine. The recent study examined a wider range of microbes near the glacier's end, including eukaryotes: organisms whose cells contain a membrane-enclosed nucleus, unlike bacteria.[3]
The detected groups included diatoms, tiny algae with glass-like shells, as well as dinoflagellates and ciliates, other kinds of microscopic organisms.[2]

The location matters. The scientists collected red-stained ice, mud and sediment near the exposed glacier edge, where brine discharge interacts with surface conditions.[1]
The paper explicitly states that eukaryotes have not yet been detected in the underground brine itself.[1]

## How the researchers checked it

The team compared 167 samples from the glacier's edge, other Dry Valleys habitats, wind-deposited material and marine sites in nearby McMurdo Sound.[2]
That comparison let them ask whether the community around Blood Falls resembled nearby freshwater communities or organisms associated with the sea.[8]

Genetic analysis found a stronger marine connection at the glacier's end than at other Dry Valleys sites.[8]
The publisher's summary reports that 9.34% of the eukaryotic genetic variants there were shared with oceanic samples, compared with 1.15% at the other aquatic sites.[8]
A genetic variant is a distinct version of a DNA sequence. Those figures measure overlap in the sampled variants, not the proportion of every organism that came from an ancient ocean.

The researchers also examined RNA, molecular material that can reveal which genes cells are using.[2]
They detected activity associated with photosynthesis, responses to salt stress and cellular repair.[2]
This provided evidence of active organisms, rather than only traces left by dead microbes.[2]
The light-using activity belongs to the sampled glacier-edge habitats; it is not evidence of photosynthesis deep beneath opaque ice.[1]

Modern wind transport alone appeared unlikely to explain the marine pattern: marine signatures were scarce in the wind-collected samples.[8]
The results support a past connection with the sea.[7]

## Animals beneath floating ice

An ice shelf is floating ice extending from the land over the sea. Water and seabed lie beneath it, so it is not the same setting as a brine reservoir under an inland glacier.[4]

On the 13th of January 2025, iceberg A-84 broke away from the George VI Ice Shelf near the Antarctic Peninsula.[4]
An international team aboard the research vessel *Falkor (too)* reached the exposed area on the 25th of January and changed its plans to investigate.[4]
Schmidt Ocean Institute, which operates the vessel, announced the findings on the 20th of March 2025.[4]

The team used SuBastian, a remotely operated underwater vehicle, to survey the seabed over eight days. It recorded large corals and sponges alongside icefish, giant sea spiders and octopus, with observations extending to depths of 1,300 metres.[4]
The survey began after the ice detached, so it provides a view of recently uncovered seabed rather than conditions under an intact shelf.[4]

There is also evidence from beneath ice that remained in place. A paper published in February 2021 described animals attached to a boulder beneath the Filchner Ice Shelf.[6]
Researchers lowered a camera through a borehole drilled through 872 metres of ice, at a site about 260 kilometres from the shelf's open-water edge.[6]
They observed sponges and other attached organisms, but could not identify all of them from the footage.[6]
The authors said that identifying species would require physical specimens and genetic material.[6]

## Why these findings matter

At Blood Falls, the biological evidence offers another way to investigate the area's environmental history. The organisms' marine connections can be compared with earlier chemical evidence that the brine originated as seawater.[7]
The finding concerns how a community may persist and change after an environment becomes separated from the ocean; it is not proof of an unchanged ancient community.[2]

The marine animals raise a different question: how does enough food reach seabed covered by ice? Cambridge's account of the A-84 expedition explains that the usual supply falling from surface waters would have been blocked by the ice overhead.[5]
Researchers propose that currents carried food into the area from elsewhere, but the precise route and supply mechanism remain unresolved.[5]

## What is still unclear

The Blood Falls study does not settle when the brine became isolated or the complete history of its present community.[7]
Yale's account also cautions against treating the newly identified microbes as proven descendants of organisms trapped millions of years ago.[3]

For the A-84 site, the expedition reported suspected new species, not a completed identification of all the animals.[4]
How conditions change after the ice breaks away remains a research question.[5]
