European Union (EU) governments remain divided over who should benefit from proposed “Made in EU” rules, after industry ministers met in Brussels on the 24th of September.[31][55]
France wants a tighter preference for production inside the bloc; Germany wants trusted trading partners included; Spain is trying to bridge the gap.[1][55]

The dispute concerns the Industrial Accelerator Act, a proposal to use public contracts and support schemes to strengthen European manufacturing.[11]
For a factory competing for publicly funded orders, the definition of “European” could matter rather more than the flag outside its headquarters. But this is still a negotiation, not a new rule already governing purchases: the European Parliament’s procedure remains at the committee stage.[10]

## What the proposal would change

The European Commission, the EU body that proposes legislation, presented the act on the 4th of March.[12]
It would combine European-production and lower-carbon requirements in selected strategic sectors with faster industrial permits and conditions on certain large foreign investments.[12]
Public procurement simply means public bodies buying goods, services or construction work.[12]
Here, that spending would help create demand for particular industrial products rather than leave price as the only consideration.[11]

The details are not uniform. The Commission proposes low-carbon requirements for steel used in covered automotive and construction spending, while cement and aluminium face both origin and low-carbon provisions in specified uses.[11]
Batteries, solar equipment and other clean technologies have their own proposed origin requirements.[11]
There is no single European-content percentage for everything on the shopping list.[11]

That distinction matters: this is not a general ban on imported cars or equipment.[4]
Products that fail to qualify could still be sold, but could miss out on the relevant public support or preferential treatment.[4]
The immediate question is where public money goes, not whether every household must buy European.

## Where governments disagree

The latest disagreement is about how wide to draw the circle. Euronews reports that Germany wants reciprocal access: partners could benefit if European companies receive equivalent opportunities in their markets.[1]
That would make it easier to retain supply chains crossing the EU’s external borders, rather than treating every non-member as the same kind of competitor.

| Position | What it would prioritise |
|---|---|
| France | Public money supporting factories and workers within the EU.[55] |
| Germany | European production working with trusted partners, including countries such as Norway, Switzerland and Canada.[1] |
| Spain | A tiered approach: the EU at the centre, then closer or reciprocal partners, then a wider group linked by trade arrangements.[1] |

Spain’s proposal also envisages allowing critical components from outside where sufficient EU manufacturing capacity or alternative supplies are unavailable, according to Euronews.[1]
That is an attempt to reconcile the desire for more factories with the awkward business of getting products made before those factories exist.

The official meeting account describes an update on the act’s progress.[31]
Ministers’ formal policy debate on the separate Chips Act 2.0 should not be confused with approval of these broader manufacturing rules.[31]

## The argument beyond the flag

The European Economic and Social Committee, an EU advisory body representing employers, workers and civil society, supports the act’s direction but wants stronger safeguards for industry, fair competition and quality jobs.[2]
Its warning is practical: quicker permits will achieve little without dependable energy, infrastructure, skilled workers and better coordination between European and national rules.[2]
A preference in a tender cannot, by itself, supply a factory with electricity or train its next shift.

At a September roundtable, employers’ representative Sandra Parthie argued for investment in energy, transport and telecommunications infrastructure, linking competitive production to lower energy costs and modernised grids.[9]
Workers’ representative Florian Marin stressed skills, innovation, decent working conditions and collective bargaining rather than trying to win international competition by cutting wages.[9]
Those positions share an interest in investment, while differing over what must be protected along the way.

A parliamentary hearing exposed another tension. EU Perspectives reported that Volkswagen supported linking public help to European employment, while the manufacturing union industriAll wanted assistance tied to investment, employment and environmental conditions.[4]
The question is not simply whether a company receives support, but what it does in return. Keeping production, improving skills and protecting jobs are more concrete tests than putting a European label on an application form.

There are costs to consider, too.
The European Parliament’s research service records concerns from business organisations about practical local-content rules and from environmental groups about whether faster industrial development would retain adequate safeguards.[12]
Supporting manufacturing, keeping procurement affordable and accelerating cleaner production are related goals, not automatically identical ones.

## What it means for trading partners

The partner-country argument is already affecting relations with Britain. The PA news agency reported on the 22nd of September that the UK government had raised concerns about damage to British industry and supply chains in talks with the Commission president.[23]
That is a warning about potential consequences, not evidence that British businesses have already been excluded by a finished law.

Nor does having a trade agreement settle every detail. The parliamentary briefing distinguishes between eligibility in public procurement and other support schemes, with conditions and possible exclusions under the proposal.[12]
The argument therefore reaches beyond geography: it is about which commitments countries make to one another and which activities those commitments cover.

## What is still undecided

The final definition of eligible production, treatment of partner countries and negotiated sector rules remain unresolved.[55]
Parliament and member governments still have work to do before there is a final act.[10][55]
A political ambition to make progress is not the same thing as an agreed start date for businesses.

The Commission’s aim is to lift manufacturing from 14.3% of EU economic output in 2024 to 20% by 2035.[11]
That is a target, not a measured result or a promise that this legislation alone will deliver it. The sources reviewed here do not establish what the eventual package would add to prices or how many jobs it would preserve.

## Dadbot take

“Made in EU” sounds like a label. The useful test is what it buys: additional production, dependable supplies and worthwhile jobs, at a cost the public can understand. A narrow definition could sharpen the incentive to build inside the bloc; a broader one could protect valuable partnerships. Neither design answers the harder question of whether supported businesses become stronger. The small print deserves at least as much attention as the slogan.
