Schools across Italy, Scotland, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland received hoax bomb threats on the 11th of November 2026, prompting evacuations and police enquiries.[7]
Hundreds of Italian schools were evacuated, while police in Scotland and both Irish jurisdictions assessed the messages as hoaxes or not credible.[5][7]

For families, the interruption meant collecting children, waiting for updates and finding out whether lessons would continue.[4][5][6]
The response varied between schools: some sent pupils home, others reopened after checks, and many continued teaching.[2][3][6]

## Italy: thousands of inboxes, hundreds of evacuations

Italian officials told the BBC that a single email appeared to have reached the inboxes of 6,300 of the country's 7,476 state schools.[3]
Private schools were unaffected, according to those officials.[3]
Reuters reported that at least 300 schools were evacuated after receiving warnings.[5]

In Rome, one school used a messaging app to tell parents that pupils had been moved to a safe area and were being supervised by teaching staff.[5]
Parents left work or changed their plans to collect children.[5]
Nensi Bego, whose daughter attends Mazzini middle school in Rome, told Reuters that her daughter had cried because the children did not understand what was happening and remained shaken when collected.[5]

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said checks had found no evidence of concrete danger.[7]
Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara described the incidents as probably a cyberattack.[3]

Some schools resumed lessons after the alert was lifted.[3]
The BBC reported that most schools in Milan did not send pupils home, although some parents chose to collect their children.[3]
The head of the Umbria regional government, Stefania Proietti, said checks in the region's schools had proved negative and police were examining each report.[3]

## Scotland: police advice and precautionary evacuations

At least 1,000 Scottish schools received the email, according to BBC Scotland, with both primary and secondary schools affected.[2]
Local authorities confirmed messages had reached schools in Glasgow, Edinburgh, East Lothian, Aberdeenshire and Orkney.[2]

Police Scotland said the threats were assessed immediately and treated as hoaxes.[1]
Its enquiries found nothing suggesting a credible threat or a wider danger to the public.[1]
The force advised that the email's contents alone did not require schools to evacuate or children to be kept away from education.[1]

Some schools had already taken precautions.[2]
Queensferry High School in Edinburgh evacuated and sent pupils home, while schools in Dumfries and Galloway also evacuated.[2]
Highland Council said several schools activated their emergency procedures.[2]
Most councils contacted by BBC Scotland said schools were operating normally.[2]
Glasgow council emailed parents to say police had confirmed the threat was a hoax and schools should not be evacuated.[7]
In Edinburgh, the council said schools had briefly evacuated as a precaution and told parents there was no need to collect their children.[7]

Assistant Chief Constable Mark Sutherland told the BBC that police had assessed the threat as a hoax in around an hour.[2]
Education Secretary Mairi McAllan met police and local authorities and told the Scottish Parliament that specialist officers were working to identify the source.[2]

## Northern Ireland: schools told to stay open

About 300 schools were affected in Northern Ireland, where the Police Service of Northern Ireland, or PSNI, treated the message as a hoax.[7]
The Department of Education advised schools to remain open unless police directed otherwise.[7]

Victoria Primary School in Newtownards, County Down, evacuated in the morning and reopened about an hour later following departmental guidance.[6]
Parent Carmen Hill told the BBC that pupils had been taken to a nearby church, and that she collected her daughter before taking her back once the school reopened.[6]

At St John the Baptist Primary School in Belfast, principal Chris Donnelly said classrooms were checked and parents, guardians and school governors contacted.[6]

Education Minister Paul Givan held an urgent meeting with police and the Education Authority.[6]
He said he wanted non-credible threats to be ruled out faster and better email screening to stop such messages reaching headteachers.[6]

## Republic of Ireland: calls, searches and parent messages

Irish police, known as gardaí, assessed the emailed threat as not credible and did not call for evacuations.[7]
RTÉ reported that schools in Dublin, Cork and Wicklow were believed to have been targeted.[4]

Prime Minister Micheál Martin told the Irish Parliament that police had received several hundred calls from schools about the emailed warnings.[4]
He said officers would contact each school, although the volume of calls meant that could take time.[4]

Education Minister Hildegarde Naughton said police had assessed the threat as not credible and advised that schools did not need to evacuate.[4]
RTÉ reported that some schools evacuated pupils or carried out precautionary searches, while many contacted parents and guardians.[4]

The police response involved assessing each reported threat and then advising the school on what to do.[4]
Deputy Commissioner Paul Cleary had set out that process the previous day, saying the police control centre would assess credibility before the centre or responding officers gave instructions.[4]

## What investigators are examining

Police Scotland's Cyber and Fraud Unit is investigating the source of the messages and working with law enforcement partners across the UK and internationally.[1]
The force said the emails had been sent widely to distribution lists.[1]

Sutherland told the BBC that officers believed the Scottish messages came from the same source as those sent to Northern Ireland and elsewhere in the UK; he said it was too early to identify who was responsible.[2]
The PSNI said the email it was investigating originated outside the UK.[6]

The BBC examined the emails sent in Italy and Scotland and reported similar wording and the same purported sender's name, accompanied by different group descriptions.[3]
Its European report said the connection between the incidents remained unclear.[3]

The alerts followed earlier school hoaxes in October and bomb warnings that disrupted European aviation the previous week.[2][3][6]
Scotland's previous school alert was on the 19th of October.[2]
