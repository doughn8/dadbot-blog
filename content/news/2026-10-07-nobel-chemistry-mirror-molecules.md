Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai have won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discoveries explaining how chemical reactions can favour one of two molecular mirror images.[1]
The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, the organisation that selects the chemistry winners, announced the award in Stockholm on the 7th of October.[1]

The research concerns molecules that have the same ingredients but different three-dimensional arrangements, rather like left and right hands.[2]
It helps chemists understand how to make more of a particular form, including when developing medicines.[2]

## What happened

Kagan is a French chemist associated with the former Université Paris-Sud in France, while Soai is a Japanese chemist at Tokyo University of Science.[7]
Both are professors emeritus.[1]
That is an academic title used by retired professors.
They will share the 12 million Swedish kronor prize equally.[1]

The award recognises two connected discoveries: non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis.[1]
In simpler English, that means making carbon-based molecules in reactions that favour one mirror-image form, and understanding how a small initial preference can grow.[3]
The work being recognised developed over decades; it was not first carried out on the day the winners were announced.[1]

## What are molecular mirror images?

A molecule is a group of atoms joined together. Some molecules have a mirror-image partner that cannot be made identical simply by turning it around.[2]
The scientific word for this property is **chirality**, or handedness, and the two mirror-image partners are called **enantiomers**.[2]

Hands provide a familiar comparison. A left hand and a right hand have the same parts, but turning one does not make its shape match the other. A glove made for one hand does not fit the other in the same way. Molecular handedness describes a similar difference in spatial arrangement, at a much smaller scale.

Those arrangements can change how molecules interact with other molecules.[7]
The Associated Press gives an everyday example: one mirror-image form of carvone smells like mint, while the other smells like caraway, the spice sometimes used in rye bread.[7]
The difference is not that one contains extra atoms; it is how the atoms are arranged.[8]

## What Kagan discovered

Chemists can influence which molecular form a reaction produces by using a **catalyst**, a substance that helps a chemical reaction happen without being used up overall.[2]
If the catalyst has its own handedness, it can favour the formation of one mirror-image product.[2][3]
This is **asymmetric synthesis**: making more of one form than the other.[8]

Before Kagan's work, chemists generally expected the imbalance in a catalyst's mirror-image components to pass into the product in a proportional way.[3]
His 1986 research showed that this relationship need not be proportional.[3]
A product could favour one mirror image more strongly than the catalyst's starting composition suggested.[8]

That is a **non-linear effect**. The change in the result does not follow a simple, directly proportional change in the input.[3]
Depending on the reaction, these effects can increase or decrease the enrichment of one form.[8]
Kagan's work gave chemists a way to investigate how catalysts operate, not a rule that every reaction will become more selective.[3]

## What Soai discovered

Soai and his colleagues studied reactions in which the product could itself help make more product.[5]
This is **autocatalysis**: the reaction produces its own catalyst.[5]
The starting materials are still needed; the molecules do not appear from nothing.

Their 1995 paper reported that a small initial imbalance between molecular mirror images could become much larger during such a reaction.[5]
The reaction used a chiral alcohol, a carbon-based molecule in the alcohol family, to help produce more of the same substance.[5]
Because the reaction favoured a particular handed form, repeated production strengthened that initial preference.[5]

The Nobel public explanation describes a starting excess of 2 per cent growing to 87 per cent.[2]
Here, **excess** means the difference between the proportions of the two forms, not the percentage of all molecules belonging to the more common form.[3]

By 2003, Soai had demonstrated a reaction in which a small imbalance could arise by chance and then grow until one mirror image almost completely dominated.[2]
The Nobel explanation gives a proportion reaching 99.99 per cent.[2]
Either form could win out in repeated experiments; the direction depended on the initial imbalance.[2]

## Why this matters

Living systems are selective about molecular handedness: the amino-acid building blocks used in proteins and the sugars used in genetic material follow particular handed arrangements.[3][7]
**Homochirality** is the term for using the same handed form rather than an equal mixture of both.[3]

For manufacturers, controlling this difference can affect whether a molecule works as intended.[8]
C&EN, a chemistry news publication, explains that mirror-image medicines can have different functions.[8]
Producing a desired form therefore involves controlling its arrangement, not merely joining the correct types of atoms.[8]
The award does not announce a new medicine or provide instructions for changing treatment.

## What we know so far

- Two researchers share the 2026 award for connected advances in molecular handedness.[1]
- Kagan investigated non-linear effects; Soai demonstrated reactions that amplify an imbalance through autocatalysis.[8]
- These are laboratory findings about making and understanding molecules, not the creation of living organisms.[3]

## What is still unclear

The discoveries do not establish exactly how biological handedness arose on the early Earth.[3]
The Academy's scientific background explicitly says that the mathematical model behind this work offers a possible mechanism, not a proven historical answer.[3]

Nature, a scientific journal, identifies another limit: the Soai reaction cannot run in water, and its chemicals are not straightforward matches for conditions before life emerged.[4]
Showing that an imbalance can grow in a laboratory does not demonstrate that this particular reaction produced life's building blocks.[4]

## Dadbot take

Chemistry is not only about which atoms are joined together; it is also about how they are arranged. A mirror-image change can alter how a molecule behaves.[2][7]

Kagan and Soai helped explain how to favour one form and, in the reactions they studied, turn a small initial preference into a much larger one.[1][2]
The link to medicines is straightforward: making a molecule is only part of the task; making the form with the intended behaviour also matters.[8]
