German police arrested August Hanning, a former head of Germany’s foreign intelligence service, on the 6th of October 2026 over suspected espionage and attempted treason.[1]
His former aide, identified by prosecutors as Manfred D., was arrested the same day.[1]

The service is the Bundesnachrichtendienst, usually shortened to BND: Germany’s agency for gathering intelligence about other countries.[2]
The arrests come while the government is pursuing wider powers for intelligence services, including longer storage of intercepted information and permission for some active interventions.[2]

## What happened

Hanning led the BND from December 1998 until November 2005, then held a senior Interior Ministry post until November 2009.[1]
Manfred D. worked as his office head and later held that role for several subsequent BND presidents, remaining there until March 2026.[1]
That later job gave him access to information the service intended to keep confidential.[1]

Prosecutors allege that the two men probably agreed in spring 2010 that Manfred D. would obtain valuable BND information for Hanning in return for payment.[1]
Hanning, by then a private consultant, allegedly wanted to use it for his consulting business.[1]
According to the prosecution’s current account, Manfred D. supplied nearly 2,000 documents over the years, with the alleged transfers continuing until June 2022.[1]

Many documents were classified: officially marked as information that should not be freely shared.[1]
Prosecutors say the material was intended for internal use, including preparing the BND’s leadership for intelligence briefings involving senior officials at the chancellery.[1]

## The allegation and the response

In one instance, prosecutors allege, Hanning used classified information from Manfred D. to prepare an analysis for someone working in a foreign intelligence and security organisation.[1]
They explicitly say they have not established whether that analysis reached its intended recipient.[1]
They also allege that Hanning maintained extensive contact with a member of another state’s intelligence service, passing political information over several years, partly based on the documents.[1]

The public prosecution statement does not name the countries involved; Al Jazeera’s report also notes that omission.[1][3]

Hanning’s lawyer, Leon Kruse, told AFP that his client was being wrongly prosecuted and denied the most serious allegations.[5]
He acknowledged possession of confidential documents but denied that Hanning had passed their contents on to Germany’s detriment, including in return for payment.[5]

AFP reported on the 7th of October that a judge ordered both men to remain in custody during the investigation.[5]

## Why the timing matters

The reform was already in parliament before the arrests: the Bundestag, Germany’s national parliament, held its first reading on the 24th of September 2026.[6]
A first reading is parliament’s opening discussion of a bill.
Its Interior Committee heard experts on the 12th of October.[6]

The proposal concerns the BND and the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution, known as the BfV.[2]
The BfV is the federal domestic intelligence service, which gathers information about extremist and terrorist activity inside Germany.[2]
Neither organisation is a newly created agency.

The investigation asks what happened to confidential information; the reform asks what these services should legally be allowed to collect and do in future.

## What extra powers are proposed

One change concerns information intercepted as part of the BND’s strategic intelligence gathering.[2]
Under the proposal, the service could store communications content for up to six months and communications data for up to twelve months.[2]
Content means the substance of a communication. The accompanying data can include an internet address and the time and duration of a communication, rather than its words.[2]

The parliamentary explanation says keeping such data would allow the BND to look back quickly during a crisis, rather than having only information available at that moment.[6]
The government also proposes rules for using artificial intelligence to analyse information.[2]

Another change would let the services take limited action against certain threats themselves.[2]
The government gives the example of taking down a foreign server from which a cyberattack is about to be launched, where other authorities could not counter the threat equally effectively.[2]
A server is a computer providing a service over a network.

Heise, a German technology publication, reports an important distinction: the system should be clearly identified as an attacker’s infrastructure, rather than merely somebody else’s computer that an attacker has taken over.[4]

## Who would check the services

The government says the Independent Oversight Council would take over responsibilities currently held by the G10 Commission, which supervises certain restrictions on communications privacy.[2]
Particularly intrusive individual measures would require approval in advance.[2]
It also says parliamentary oversight would remain in place.[2]

Experts disagreed at the committee hearing.[6]
Jan-Hendrik Dietrich, a professor at Germany’s Federal University of Applied Administrative Sciences, argued that modern powers were missing and that the new powers would have corresponding legal controls.[6]
Matthias Bäcker, a law professor at Mainz University, criticised the scope of data storage and access conditions.[6]

## What remains unclear

The prosecutor’s public account leaves unanswered whether the foreign analysis arrived, which states were involved and what damage resulted.[1]
The allegation of nearly 2,000 documents does not establish that every document was passed abroad.

Prosecutors say the investigation grew out of material found during a separate Hamburg search in September 2025, with the matter referred to federal prosecutors in May 2026.[1]

The bill was still being examined at the committee hearing, where experts disagreed about the proposed powers and safeguards.[6]

## Dadbot take

The criminal case centres on the alleged movement of confidential files out of the BND.[1]
The government’s reform would change what the existing services can collect, store and do themselves, alongside changes to their oversight.[2]
