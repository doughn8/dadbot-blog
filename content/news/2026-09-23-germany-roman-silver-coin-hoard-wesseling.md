A licensed metal detectorist helped uncover 934 Roman silver coins near Wesseling, south of Cologne — a hoard the LVR describes as Germany’s largest known Hadrian-era find.[1][2]

The coins weigh 3.1 kilograms.[1][2]

They range from 148 BC to AD 124/125, and archaeologists think they were buried around AD 135.[1][2]

The find was announced publicly on the 22nd of September 2026.[1][2]

## How the coins were found and dug up

Oliver Riedl was searching farmland near Wesseling with permission in October 2025 when his detector began producing signals.[1][2][3]

He found one silver coin after another.[1][3]

After 15 coins had been recovered, the detector was still indicating more below the surface.[1][2]

Riedl contacted the LVR’s archaeological office the following Monday, and a professional excavation team arrived shortly afterwards.[2][3]

The first excavation recovered most of the 934 coins.[3]

Because the field was still being farmed, archaeologists waited until the land could be examined again.[3]

They returned in July and August 2026 for a follow-up dig to examine the deposit’s wider setting.[3]

The LVR team recovered the coins as a block and took them to the restoration workshops at the LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn.[1]

There, specialists X-rayed the block before carefully separating and exposing the individual coins.[1]

The block contained more than silver.[1]

Archaeologists found pieces of a ceramic vessel and traces of straw.[1]

The LVR says the coins were probably placed in a ceramic container padded with straw for protection.[1]

Agricultural activity later destroyed the vessel and spread the coins through the topsoil.[1]

All of the coins were recovered.[1]

The follow-up work also checked the wider landscape.[3]

A Roman villa rustica — a rural estate — is known about 250 to 300 metres north of the find.[3]

The local report says no definite connection has been established, so the villa remains context rather than an explanation for the hoard.[3]

The detector found the concentration, but the archaeological recovery established how the coins had been stored.[1][3]

## What was found

The hoard consists of Roman denarii, small silver coins.[1]

The oldest dates to 148 BC and the youngest to AD 124/125, so the group contains coins made more than 270 years apart.[1][2]

The LVR calls it the largest known Hadrian-era coin hoard in Germany and the fifth-largest known hoard of its kind worldwide.[1]

That is a defined comparison, not a claim that this is the largest ancient silver hoard in Germany or the largest Roman hoard of every type.[1]

## What the coins can tell us

The dates describe the coins, not a complete record of who held them.[1][2]

Archaeologists estimate that the coins were probably buried around AD 135.[1][2]

The LVR and Deutsche Welle also use the comparison that the silver represented roughly six years of pay for a Roman legionary.[1][2]

That gives the hoard a sense of scale; it does not identify the person who gathered or buried it.[1][2]

The evidence establishes how the deposit was recovered and what was found with it.[1][3]

It does not establish why the money was buried or why nobody retrieved it.[1][3]

The owner and motive remain unknown because the evidence does not preserve them.[1][3]

## Where to see the coins

The hoard is being shown at the LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn from the 23rd of September 2026 to the 7th of January 2027.[1]
