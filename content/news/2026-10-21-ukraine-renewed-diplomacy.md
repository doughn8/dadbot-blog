US envoy Steve Witkoff arrived in St Petersburg on the 21st of October 2026 ahead of planned talks with Russian President Vladimir Putin, following discussions with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Oslo the previous day.[1][3]
The visit brings a fresh attempt to turn proposals into negotiations over ending Russia's war in Ukraine.[2]

There is a reason to pay attention to this round: Ukraine, the United States and European participants have been discussing what a peace framework could contain, including Ukraine's security after the fighting stops.[2]
That is useful work. For people living through the war, the point is not another photograph of people shaking hands. It is whether the next day becomes safer.

## What happened

Witkoff is US President Donald Trump's special envoy, acting as a negotiator between governments.[1]
His journey from Oslo to St Petersburg is an example of shuttle diplomacy: carrying proposals between participants rather than gathering everyone around one table.[1][2]

Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre and Finnish President Alexander Stubb joined the Oslo discussions with Zelenskyy and Witkoff; Trump's adviser Jared Kushner participated remotely, according to the Guardian.[4]
The Moscow Times, carrying Reuters reporting, says a US official described the talks as constructive and said Ukraine brought forward new proposals.[2]

Russian presidential aide Yuri Ushakov said Ukraine would be the main subject of the planned Putin meeting, according to Ukrainska Pravda.[3]
The immediate diplomatic task is therefore quite concrete: take the discussions held with Ukraine and European partners to Russia, then find out what the Russian leadership will accept.[2][3]

## Why this matters

A ceasefire and a peace settlement are different things. A ceasefire is an agreement to stop specified fighting. A wider settlement tackles the conditions for ending the conflict and what follows. The first can give people breathing space while the second is negotiated; its value depends on whether the promised pause actually happens.

Think of what a reliable pause could mean: more opportunity to repair damaged electricity supplies, move essential goods or let a family plan a day without the same immediate danger. The human value of even limited progress is worth taking seriously.

There is also a reported practical development alongside the diplomacy. Zelenskyy said Ukraine was again receiving advance warnings of Russian ballistic missile attacks after interruptions, according to the Guardian.[4]

## What we know so far

- **Security is part of the discussion.** The US official cited in the Reuters-contributed report said Oslo covered security guarantees for Ukraine and a post-war economic plan.[2]
- **The aim includes more direct negotiations.** The same official described an effort to create a route towards talks involving three parties and a wider group of countries.[2]
- **Participants sounded encouraged.** Støre called the meeting constructive, while Stubb described the peace process as back on track, according to the Moscow Times.[2]

Security guarantees are commitments intended to help protect Ukraine against future attacks.[6]
For example, the existing UK–Ukraine security agreement includes support for Ukraine's defence and measures to deter renewed Russian aggression.[6] The important detail is what those commitments require in practice: who would help, what they would do and when. A reassuring label is less useful than an answer to those questions.

## What is still unclear

The reporting reviewed here does not establish a jointly accepted ceasefire text or a Russian agreement to the Oslo proposals.[1][2][3]
Nor does it establish a settlement of the territorial and security questions, an agreed start date for stopping the fighting, or a complete system for checking compliance.[2][3]

Those gaps give the next announcements a useful test. What fighting would stop? Who would check? What happens if someone breaks the agreement? A public account of those arrangements would say more about the chances of a durable pause than another description of a good meeting.

Ukraine's place in the negotiations matters too. Zelenskyy said in Oslo that decisions about Ukraine's future would only be acceptable with Ukraine at the negotiating table, the Guardian reported.[4]
An agreement has to be something the countries expected to carry it out will actually accept.

## How the right is framing it

An earlier Fox News interview offers one example of sympathetic coverage of Trump's diplomatic approach.[5]
Published on the 27th of September, it emphasised Stubb's view that Trump's team was working hard to find a solution and his description of quiet coordination between Ukraine, the United States and European governments.[5]

That is background, not Fox reporting on this week's Oslo meeting.[5]
Its useful argument is that persistent contact deserves credit: negotiators cannot find common ground if nobody is carrying proposals between the parties.

## How the left is framing it

The Guardian's current report puts more weight on Ukraine's security, its participation in negotiations and disputes over American support.[4]
It reports Zelenskyy's concern about restrictions, but also US Secretary of State Marco Rubio's denial that there had been a deliberate political instruction to harm Ukraine.[4]

That emphasis raises a different test of diplomacy: whether efforts to secure an agreement leave Ukraine with protection it considers credible. The distinction is between getting an agreement signed and making its terms workable afterwards, not between wanting peace and opposing it.

## Where they differ

These reports have different emphases and publication dates; they are not a poll of conservative or progressive opinion.[4][5]
Giving credit for diplomatic effort and examining the terms of a possible deal answer different questions. Readers can welcome the effort and still judge the eventual agreement on its contents.
