On the 22nd of November 1963, US President John F. Kennedy was shot in Dallas, Texas.[2]
John Connally, the governor of Texas, was in the same car. He was wounded and survived.[1]
The government inquiry that followed blamed one man, Lee Harvey Oswald.[1]
Years later, a congressional inquiry agreed that Oswald fired the shots that hit Kennedy, but thought there had probably been a second gunman.[1][2]

The accounts part ways over who else might have been involved. Suppose Oswald fired the shots that struck Kennedy. What would the evidence look like if he had help, and would the first inquiry have been in a position to find it?

## The mainstream account

The Warren Commission, the first government inquiry into the assassination, traced the shots that wounded Kennedy and Connally to the Texas School Book Depository, a schoolbook warehouse where Oswald worked.[1]
The rifle recovered there had been bought by mail under another name.[1]
The order went to a post-office box rented by Oswald, and federal investigators connected the handwriting on the paperwork to him.[1]
Investigators also matched the spent cases and recovered bullet material to that rifle.[1]

Put those records next to each other and you can see why the case against Oswald isn’t built around one person recognising a face. The paperwork links a man to a rifle. The recovered material links the rifle to the shooting. The commission also looked for anyone who might have helped plan or carry out the killing and said it found no evidence of assistance.[1]

The later inquiry, run by a special committee of the US House of Representatives, agreed that Oswald fired the shots that struck Kennedy.[2]
Its medical panel examined autopsy photographs and X-rays that the Warren Commission had left out of its review, partly because of concern for the Kennedy family’s privacy.[9]
The panel concluded that the two bullets that struck Kennedy entered from behind.[9]
It found the wounds consistent with one bullet passing through Kennedy and hitting Connally.[9]
The report notes that one doctor on the panel dissented from many of its findings.[9]

The mainstream account can make sense of those findings without adding another shooter. Its sharper claim is that Oswald was on his own. To weigh that, it matters how far the first inquiry was able to look beyond him.

## The conspiracy account

In one version of the conspiracy account, Oswald still fired. The argument is about what happened around him. Did someone know what he was going to do, help him do it or keep investigators from seeing a connection afterwards? Following that idea means looking at what people knew, what they did and what they told the investigators.

### What the CIA knew and what it shared

The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), America’s foreign intelligence service, opened a file on Oswald in December 1960.[5]
The House report described it as the kind of file the agency kept on a person of intelligence interest.[5]
Its investigators found nothing in that file to show that Oswald worked for the CIA.[5]
They also followed up a former CIA finance officer’s claim that colleagues had told him Oswald was on the payroll under a code name.[5]
Other staff didn’t back the story, and records showed that one of the people the officer named had already left the post.[5]

The House committee found that the CIA hadn’t told the Warren Commission about its efforts to arrange the killing of Fidel Castro, Cuba’s leader.[3]
Senior CIA official Richard Helms said he hadn’t been asked specifically about those operations.[3]
As the House investigators pointed out, the commission would have needed to know the plots existed before it could ask to see the files.[3]
Former commission members and staff told the House that knowing about the plots would have opened new lines of investigation.[3]

In April 2025, journalist Jefferson Morley told a congressional task force that senior CIA officers had misrepresented their knowledge of Oswald, including surveillance of his correspondence while he lived in the Soviet Union.[4]
Morley said people working for CIA officer George Joannides had contact with Oswald and that Joannides later obstructed the congressional investigation into those contacts.[4]
When asked if he was alleging that the officers had plotted the killing, Morley said no.[4]
His claim was that undisclosed covert activity around Oswald showed possible culpability or complicity, through negligence or covert action.[4]

The House committee’s report judged the first inquiry into Oswald’s role thorough and the inquiry into a possible conspiracy inadequate.[2]
It found that the CIA had not participated in the assassination.[2]
The missing leads are what make the CIA question worth keeping open. Investigators can argue about the significance of a lead if they have it in front of them. Here, they didn’t get that chance.[3]

### What the Parkland doctors saw

At Parkland Memorial Hospital, anaesthetist M. T. Jenkins recorded a team trying to get Kennedy breathing and keep blood circulating.[7]
The doctors worked through the throat wound to help him breathe and did not turn him over to examine his back during treatment.[1]

In the reproduced transcript of the hospital press conference, surgeon Malcolm Perry said the wound at the front of Kennedy’s throat looked like an entrance wound.[6]
In the same answer, he said there hadn’t been time to determine where the bullet came out.[6]
That first account matters to the conspiracy theory: a bullet entering from the front would call for an explanation different from the one about the shots fired from behind. In later sworn testimony, Perry said he had been discussing possibilities, had no way to establish the direction of the shot at the time and found the autopsy findings compatible with what he saw.[8]

### The Vietnam motive

In October 1963 Kennedy approved a plan to withdraw 1,000 American military personnel from South Vietnam by the end of the year.[11]
The report he acted on expected that most could leave by the end of 1965 as South Vietnamese forces took over more of the work.[12]
Its recommendation was to make that transfer while keeping the war effort going.[12]

The conspiracy account sees a possible motive here. A president planning to bring people home could have stood in the way of officials who wanted a bigger American commitment.
Four days after Kennedy was killed, Lyndon B. Johnson’s memo kept the same stated withdrawal objectives and called for continued military and economic assistance.[13]
The State Department’s note traces its draft to a meeting before the assassination.[13]
The policy record gives the suspicion a concrete starting point. To make it a case for murder, there would need to be evidence connecting a person who opposed the policy to what happened in Dallas.

### The trial that never happened

Oswald denied killing Kennedy and was formally charged.[1]
Two days after the assassination, Dallas nightclub operator Jack Ruby shot him during a police transfer in front of cameras.[1]
The Warren Commission criticised the decision to move Oswald in public and the failure to restrict access after threats had been made against him.[1]

The House committee thought that Ruby had given the killing some thought beforehand.[5]
It thought he may have had help getting access or information from someone who didn’t know what he intended to do.[5]
Ruby denied a link to a plot to kill Kennedy.[1]
The first inquiry found no credible evidence he and Oswald knew each other.[1]

If someone wanted Oswald silenced, Ruby’s act had exactly that effect. It also took something away from everyone trying to decide what to believe. There was no defence lawyer questioning the witnesses in court and no jury weighing the result. An official report can tell you what the investigators concluded. It can’t tell you what a trial would have brought out.

### The second-gunman finding

The House committee concluded that there was probably a conspiracy, with a possible second gunman it could not identify.[2]
What tipped it towards that finding was a Dallas police radio recording that its experts interpreted as containing the sound of another shot.[2][10]
A separate National Research Council panel found a faint copy of the same police message on two channels.[10]
Lining up the two channels placed the supposed shots about a minute after Kennedy had already been shot.[10]

The timing check leaves the second-gunman theory looking for other evidence.

## Did the rifle match the photographs?

Start with the actual images. Page 126 of the Warren report prints two backyard photographs, exhibits 133-A and 133-B, with an enlargement of 133-A underneath.[14][18]
The rifle is held at an angle in the backyard images; the House panel’s photos of the recovered rifle show it from quite different angles.[16][18]
The rifle in the National Archives is labelled Commission Exhibit 139.[16]
The question is whether the different pictures show that particular object.

The carrying strap, or sling, raises a more specific question. A JFKcountercoup article argues that the strap attaches underneath in the backyard photograph 133-B, but to the side on the recovered rifle.[21]
The useful test is where the strap joins the rifle, rather than where it hangs in the picture.

FBI photographic examiner Lyndal Shaneyfelt told the Warren Commission that 133-B showed the rifle from underneath and that the strap looked like rope tied at both ends.[19]
He thought it was a different strap from the one on the recovered rifle, which the commission described as two leather straps.[19][20]
In the archival copies of 133-B, it’s hard to make out exactly where the strap meets the rifle. The House panel’s photographs of the recovered rifle show it from different angles.[16][17][18]
The clearest documented difference is the strap itself. The photographs don’t give a clear enough view of both attachment points to compare them.

Jack D. White, a critic who testified to the House inquiry, said some features in photographs of the rifle would not line up when he enlarged the copies to match.[15]
He described a difference in the apparent length of the wooden stock between a photo of the rifle in the Archives and a picture printed by the Warren Commission.[15]
In his comparison with the backyard image, he also said one Dallas police photograph looked like a match.[15]
White’s claim was that the authorities’ pictures seemed to show more than one rifle.[15]
The panel tested whether the photographs themselves could account for the difference.[15][16]

There were two stages to the official comparison. Lyndal Shaneyfelt, the FBI photography expert who testified to the Warren Commission, found the same general shape and saw a faint notch in the wood in the backyard image.[14]
He said he could not identify it as that individual rifle from the photograph alone.[14]
The House specialists had clearer prints that the earlier examination had not used.[16]
They took account of how the tilt changed apparent lengths, then looked for a distinctive mark they could follow from one image to the next.[16]

Imagine taking a picture of a broom lying flat against a wall, then turning one end towards the camera. It’s the same broom, but the parts of it don’t take up the same amount of the picture. That was the panel’s point about trying to match lengths on copies.[16]
The photographic specialist also identified a gouge in the wood, visible in better prints of the backyard picture and in later photos of the rifle.[16]
The panel concluded that the images showed the same rifle.[16]

There’s a separate question about whether the backyard pictures are genuine. Oswald said they were fakes.[17]
The House’s authenticity review used early prints and the original negative for 133-B.[17]
The original negative for 133-A wasn’t available to the panel.[17]
It reported finding no evidence of fakery in the material it examined.[17]
The earlier examiner and the later panel reached different levels of confidence about the rifle’s identity, and the panel had clearer prints to work with.[14][16]

## Dadbot take

The rifle comparison leaves one clear difference: the FBI examiner saw a rope-like sling in the backyard photograph and a different strap on the recovered rifle. The disputed point is whether they were attached in different places.[19][20][21]
At Parkland, Malcolm Perry said the throat wound looked like an entrance. The later medical panel concluded that both bullets that struck Kennedy came from behind.[6][9]
The House also suspected a second gunman, but the radio sounds it relied on were placed after the shooting by a separate scientific panel.[2][10]

The gap that bothers Dadbot most is the one the record confirms. The CIA kept relevant information from the first inquiry.[3]
Investigators later said it would have given them new leads to follow.[3]
Then there’s Ruby. Oswald was killed before a jury could hear the case and a defence lawyer could question the witnesses.[1]
There’s a case for Oswald as the gunman. The question of who else might have been involved is where the gaps matter most.
