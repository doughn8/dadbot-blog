## Topic

Saudi Arabia’s East–West Pipeline — also known as Petroline — is offline after attacks, removing the route that had been helping the world’s biggest crude exporter move oil around the Strait of Hormuz. A current Reuters report says the pipeline was still offline on 15 September and that Saudi Arabia had been rerouting about four million barrels per day through it; the possible loss of up to four per cent of global supply is a conditional export-risk scenario, not a measured loss already removed from the market.[8]

## Intended Reader

A general UK and European reader who sees crude prices, pipeline maps and “four per cent of global supply” headlines, but wants to know what has actually stopped, what can be rerouted, and when the disruption could reach fuel prices and the wider economy.

## Purpose

Explain the physical chain from attack to export risk without turning pipeline capacity into lost global production. The article should give readers a calm way to read the next update: watch the duration of the outage, the barrels actually moving through Yanbu and alternative routes, the available stocks, and any confirmed partial restart.

## Angle

The useful number is not simply “four per cent”. It is the number of days before stored barrels and alternative routes stop cushioning exports.

The story should separate four things that are being collapsed into one headline:

- **Nominal capacity:** the pipeline can carry up to seven million barrels per day, according to two regional officials cited by Associated Press; that is an engineering ceiling, not current flow.[10]
- **Recent throughput:** Reuters reporting puts the recent rerouted flow at around four million barrels per day, about four per cent of global supply.[8]
- **Stored barrels:** industry estimates put the immediate Yanbu export cushion at roughly five to seven days, while other estimates differ; these are not an audited Saudi inventory release.[13][14]
- **Actual lost supply:** the four-per-cent figure becomes a potential market loss only if exports cannot be maintained through storage, partial restart, Hormuz, Suez, Egypt or other workarounds.[8][9]

The article’s central sentence can be: **the important number has a stopwatch attached to it.**

## Why This Matters

The pipeline is not just another piece of infrastructure. It is Saudi Arabia’s overland escape route from a maritime chokepoint: the BBC describes the 1,200-kilometre line as a way to bypass the Strait of Hormuz.[12] The alternative is now under pressure at the same time as the Red Sea route is constrained and the global market has already been drawing down inventories.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration says August exports from Saudi Arabia’s Yanbu port were about half their July level and forecasts that constrained Middle Eastern flows will keep production shut-ins and inventory drawdowns elevated.[9] CNBC reports that global oil inventories have already fallen by around one billion barrels and that analysts see a five-to-seven-day inventory cushion as the near-term market test.[13]

For readers, the practical consequence is not an instant global petrol shortage. It is a higher risk of persistent crude and refined-product prices if a route outage becomes a stock-and-shipping problem rather than a short safety shutdown. The article must explain that pass-through to household prices is slower and less direct than the movement in a crude-futures headline; current UK fuel-price data must be refreshed at SEO.

## Suggested Structure

1. **Lede: the bypass is down, not the whole four per cent.** State the confirmed shutdown and current status. Make the distinction between a closed route and an observed loss of global production immediately.[8][12]
2. **Draw the route in words.** Abqaiq and the eastern Gulf coast to Yanbu on the Red Sea; explain why that route mattered while Hormuz shipping was disrupted. Keep the map simple and text-led.[12]
3. **Put the three oil numbers side by side.** Explain seven million barrels per day of capacity, roughly four million barrels per day of recent rerouted flow, and “up to four per cent” as a conditional scenario rather than a present-tense supply loss.[8][10]
4. **Start the clock.** Explain the five-to-seven-day storage estimate, identify it as an industry estimate, and show why every extra day changes the problem from logistics to exports.[13][14] Cross-check the range against the Guardian’s report that Yanbu stocks could maintain exports for five to seven days and that Egyptian ports offer only additional days.[11]
5. **Show the repair uncertainty.** AP reports a three-to-five-week repair estimate and the possibility of partial operation during repairs; Reuters sources cited by the Guardian gave estimates reaching six weeks but also allowed for an earlier partial restart.[10][11] Do not flatten these into one official timetable.
6. **Widen the frame without losing the plot.** Use the EIA’s current market context to explain why a new route disruption matters more when stocks are being drawn down and Middle Eastern flows remain constrained.[9]
7. **Explain what to watch next.** Name the useful signals: an official Aramco or Saudi Energy Ministry flow/repair update; Yanbu and Egyptian-port loadings; tanker routing through Hormuz, Suez and the Red Sea; inventory and product-spread data; and a confirmed partial restart. Re-check UK and European pump-price data at SEO rather than predicting it here.
8. **Close on duration.** Return to the stopwatch: a short precautionary shutdown is a market scare; a week-plus storage draw is a supply-management problem; a prolonged outage with no partial flow is a materially different global oil story.

## Sensitivities / Red Lines

- Never write that four per cent of global oil supply has already disappeared. Say “up to four per cent could be at risk” only with the conditional source language.[8]
- Never treat seven million barrels per day as current throughput or current lost supply. Keep capacity, recent flow and actual market loss visibly separate.[8][10]
- Never turn drones reportedly launched from Iraq into proof that Iran, Iraq, the Houthis or another actor ordered or carried out the attack. Include the investigation, denials and unresolved responsibility.[12][15]
- Keep the three-to-five-week AP estimate, the Reuters range reaching six weeks, and the possibility of partial flow as competing attributed estimates, not an official schedule.[10][11]
- Do not present anonymous buyers’, traders’ or analysts’ inventory estimates as audited Saudi stock data.[11][13][14]
- Do not predict household petrol or diesel prices from a crude-futures move. Refresh current UK and European price data later and explain the lag and tax/refining effects then.
- Do not use “global oil shortage” as shorthand for a route outage while storage, rerouting, spare capacity, demand and strategic stocks remain part of the analysis.
- Keep the tone calm, concrete and human. News stays cover-free and text-led; no hero image or cover recommendation.
- Preserve the approved target of roughly 1,000–1,250 body words. This Brief does not authorise an article draft.

## What This Article Must Not Do

- Do not claim the pipeline’s full nominal capacity was flowing immediately before the attack unless a directly checked source establishes the date and measurement.
- Do not quote a repair estimate without saying who supplied it and whether partial operation is possible.
- Do not assign responsibility for the attack beyond the status of the official allegation, investigation and denials.
- Do not turn an analyst’s price scenario into a forecast or a guaranteed pump-price outcome.
